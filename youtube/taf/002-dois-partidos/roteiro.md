## PART 2 — THE FOUNDERS' WARNING (2:00–5:00)

🎣 HOOK: George Washington's farewell is one of the most quoted documents in American history. But one section almost nobody quotes. It's about parties.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 2 — Portrait of Washington + image of the Sept. 19, 1796 newspaper page. Caption: "Published Sept. 19, 1796 — never delivered as a speech"]

September 1796. Washington has served two terms and decided not to seek a third. Fun detail: his farewell was never a speech. It was printed in a Philadelphia newspaper and reprinted across the country.

Most people remember the part about foreign entanglements. But Washington also wrote, quote, "Let me now take a more comprehensive view, and warn you in the most solemn manner against the baneful effects of the spirit of party generally."

[VISUAL: Quote card: "...warn you in the most solemn manner against the baneful effects of the spirit of party generally." — George Washington, Farewell Address, 1796]

And he didn't stop there. He said that in governments chosen by the people, the spirit of party "is seen in its greatest rankness, and is truly their worst enemy."

> "The alternate domination of one faction over another, sharpened by the spirit of revenge, natural to party dissension... is itself a frightful despotism." — George Washington, 1796.

[VISUAL: Quote card, phrase "alternate domination of one faction over another" highlighted in red]

💬 RELATABLE: Read that again slowly. One side takes over. Then the other side takes over and gets even. Then the first side gets even back. Sound familiar?

You've probably seen another line online, about "cunning, ambitious, and unprincipled men." It's real — but Washington wrote it about groups obstructing the laws, not about parties directly. His warning on parties is strong enough without stretching it.

[VISUAL: Small fact-check card: "'Cunning, ambitious, and unprincipled men' — real quote, but about 'combinations and associations' obstructing the laws, not parties directly"]

James Madison worried about the same thing. In Federalist No. 10, in 1787, he wrote about "factions" — groups united by an interest that works against other citizens or the common good. His conclusion was that "the causes of faction cannot be removed," so the cure was to control their effects — a country so big and so diverse that no single faction could take over.

[VISUAL: Quote card: "The causes of faction cannot be removed... relief is only to be sought in the means of controlling its effects." — James Madison, Federalist No. 10, 1787]

The irony? A few years later, Madison helped Thomas Jefferson organize one of the first American parties. That's how strong the pull is.

### How Two Parties Took Over

🎣 HOOK: Here's a number to sit with: more than 170 years.

The Republican Party was founded in 1854. The Democrats were already there. Since then, every single president has come from one of those two parties. Every House majority. Every Senate majority.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 3 — Timeline 1789–2026: Federalists vs. Democratic-Republicans → Whigs collapse → 1854 Republican Party founded → 1860 Lincoln → every president since: D or R. Label: "172 years, same two parties"]

The Civil War. Two World Wars. Civil rights. The internet. And through all of it — the same two parties.

That's not an accident. It's a combination of math, law, money, and maps. Let's go through each one.

## PART 3 — THE MATH (5:00–8:30)

🎣 HOOK: There's a rule in political science — they actually call it a "law" — that explains why winner-take-all voting tends to leave you with two choices. Once you understand it, you'll see every election differently.

In 1951, a French political scientist named Maurice Duverger described something that sounds obvious once you hear it. When you elect one person per district, and whoever gets the most votes wins, the system tends to favor two parties. Because voting for a third option feels like throwing your vote away.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 4 — Simple diagram: "One seat + most votes wins" → "Voters fear wasting their vote" → "Votes pile onto the top two" → "Two-party system". Footnote: "Duverger, Les partis politiques, 1951 — a tendency, not an iron law"]

It's a tendency, not an iron law — the UK and Canada use a similar system and still have several parties in parliament. But in the US, with a presidential race on top, the pull is especially strong.

It's called the spoiler effect.

💬 RELATABLE: You like a third candidate more than either major party. But you're afraid voting for them just helps the candidate you like least. So you hold your nose and vote for the lesser evil. Again.

That fear is measurable. In 2025, Gallup found 57% of Americans were very concerned about wasting their vote on a third-party candidate — and 59% about helping their least-favorite candidate win.

[VISUAL: Two big numbers, Gallup, Sept. 2025: "57% — worried about wasting their vote" | "59% — worried about helping the candidate they like least"]

The most famous example is the 2000 election. In Florida, Ralph Nader of the Green Party got 97,488 votes. George W. Bush won Florida — and with it, the presidency — by 537 votes.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 5 — Florida 2000, certified results: Bush 2,912,790 (48.85%) | Gore 2,912,253 (48.84%) | Nader 97,488 (1.63%). Big label: "Margin: 537 votes". Inset, national popular vote (FEC): Gore 48.38% | Bush 47.87% | Nader 2.74%]

Whether Nader changed the outcome is still debated — some of his voters would have stayed home or picked Bush. And it cuts both ways: in 1912, a third-party run split the Republican side. But the lesson from 2000 stuck: a third-party vote is a risk you can't afford.

> Once voters believe a third-party vote is wasted, they stop casting it. Once they stop, third parties never grow. And because they never grow, voters keep believing the vote is wasted. It's a loop. And it's built into how we count votes.

### The Electoral College Multiplies It

In 48 states plus Washington, D.C., the candidate who wins the state takes all of its electoral votes — even if they win by one vote. Only Maine and Nebraska split theirs by congressional district.

[VISUAL: US map: 48 states + DC in one tone labeled "winner-take-all", Maine and Nebraska highlighted "split by district"]

So in theory, a candidate could get 20% of the vote in every single state and end up with zero electoral votes.

In 2016, the Libertarian candidate, Gary Johnson, got about 4.5 million votes. The Green candidate, Jill Stein, got about 1.5 million. Together, nearly 6 million Americans. Electoral votes for either of them: zero.

[VISUAL: Bars, FEC 2016: Johnson 4,489,221 (3.28%) + Stein 1,457,216 (1.07%) = 5.95 million votes → "Electoral votes: 0". Small text: "2024: all third-party & independent candidates combined ≈ 2.1% of the vote, 0 electoral votes"]

💬 RELATABLE: Nearly 6 million people — more than the population of the city of Los Angeles. Whatever you think of those candidates, that's a lot of voices with nowhere to land.

## PART 4 — THE RULES (8:30–13:00)

🎣 HOOK: This is where it goes from frustrating to genuinely surprising. Because it's not only math. There are written rules that make it harder for anyone new to compete.

### Getting on the Ballot

Ballot rules are mostly written by state legislatures. And almost every state legislator in the country is a Democrat or a Republican.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 6 — Stat card: "State legislators: nearly all are D or R" + gavel icon]

Run for president as an independent, and you qualify state by state, each with its own numbers and deadlines.

- In Texas in 2024, an independent needed 113,151 valid signatures, collected in a window of about ten weeks, and only from voters who hadn't voted in either party's primary that year.

- In California, the number was 219,403 signatures.

- Some states have eased up. Georgia now asks an independent presidential candidate for 7,500 signatures.

[VISUAL: Table, 2024 independent presidential petitions: California 219,403 | Texas 113,151 (Mar 5–May 13; signers can't have voted in a primary) | Georgia 7,500. Source line: CA & TX Secretaries of State, NASS]

The major parties' nominees skip all that. They get ballot lines automatically, because their parties clear the vote thresholds that keep a party on the ballot — every single time.

💬 RELATABLE: It's like the two biggest teams in a league helping write the entry rules for every new team. Even if nobody's cheating, you can guess how those rules look.

🎣 HOOK: And when a third candidate gets close, the major party that feels threatened often goes to court.

In 2004, a Pennsylvania court threw out nearly two-thirds of Ralph Nader's 51,000 signatures, kept him off the ballot, and ordered him to pay more than $80,000 in costs. In 2020, Republican candidates and groups in Texas sued to remove 44 Libertarians; the state Supreme Court said they filed too late. In 2024, Democratic Party lawyers in Georgia challenged several third-party candidates; the state Supreme Court ruled that votes for two of them wouldn't count, over their electors' paperwork.

[VISUAL: Three case cards, neutral styling: "2004 — Pennsylvania: Nader off ballot, ordered to pay $80,000+ in costs" | "2020 — Texas: Republican suit vs. 44 Libertarians, dismissed as too late" | "2024 — Georgia: Democratic challenges; Supreme Court rules votes for 2 candidates won't count (paperwork defect)"]

> Notice the pattern. It's not one party. It's whichever major party thinks the newcomer will take its votes. Sometimes the challenges find real paperwork problems. Either way, the newcomer spends months and money defending its spot on the ballot — while the major parties spend theirs campaigning.

### The Money Wall

Since 1976, there's been a public fund for presidential campaigns — the three-dollar box on your tax return. A party whose candidate won 25% or more last time is a major party, and its nominee can get the full grant. For 2024, about $123.5 million.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 7 — Two tracks: "Major party (25%+ last time): full grant — $123.5M in 2024" | "Minor party (5%–25% last time): partial grant" | "Under 5%: nothing up front". Source: FEC]

A minor party qualifies only if its candidate got at least 5% last time. In the last forty years, only Ross Perot cleared that bar — in 1992 and 1996. That's why the Reform Party he founded got about $12.6 million for the 2000 race.

The twist? Since 2008, no major-party nominee has even taken the grant, because they can raise far more on their own. According to OpenSecrets, the 2024 federal elections cost an estimated $15.9 billion.

[VISUAL: Big number "$15,900,000,000" — "Estimated cost of the 2024 federal elections (OpenSecrets)". Small text: "Last major-party nominee to accept the public grant: 2008 (FEC)"]

So the public money sits there for the parties that don't need it, mostly out of reach for the ones that do. The real currency is private fundraising on a scale no newcomer can match.

### The Debate Stage

🎣 HOOK: Now the debates. This one surprises almost everybody.

The September 2024 presidential debate drew about 67 million TV viewers. If you're not on that stage, most voters never seriously consider you.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 8 — Debate stage photo. Counter: "67.1 million TV viewers — Sept. 10, 2024 (Nielsen)"]

From 1976 to 1984, the debates were sponsored by the League of Women Voters. In 1988, the League walked away. Its president, Nancy Neuman, said the demands of the two campaigns "would perpetrate a fraud on the American voter."

[VISUAL: Quote card: "...the demands of the two campaign organizations would perpetrate a fraud on the American voter." — League of Women Voters, Oct. 3, 1988]

The group that took over was the Commission on Presidential Debates — a private nonprofit set up in 1987, led from the start by the then-chairmen of the Republican and Democratic national committees, and funded over the years by corporate sponsors.

In 1992, Ross Perot made it into all three debates and finished with almost 19% of the vote. In 1996, the Commission left him out. And in 2000, it adopted the rule it still uses: 15% in national polls.

[VISUAL: Timeline: "1976–84 League of Women Voters" → "1987 Commission founded by RNC + DNC chairs" → "1992 Perot debates, gets 18.9%" → "1996 Perot excluded" → "2000 15% rule adopted" → "2024 campaigns bypass the Commission"]

To be fair, the League itself used a 15% test in 1980, when it invited independent John Anderson. But critics point out it's hard to reach 15% if voters have never seen you on that stage.

And in 2024, the two campaigns skipped the Commission entirely and negotiated debates directly with CNN and ABC. CNN's rules: 15% in four national polls, and enough ballot lines to win 270 electoral votes. No third candidate qualified.

> Who decides who gets on the most-watched political stage in the country? The two parties, the two campaigns, or the networks they negotiate with. Not the voters.

## PART 5 — WHO PAYS THE PRICE (13:00–16:00)

🎣 HOOK: So what does all this actually cost you? It's more than "I don't like my choices."

Start with the primary. In a district that reliably votes one way, November is basically settled. The real contest is the primary — and very few people vote in it. The Bipartisan Policy Center says that in midterm primaries, about 80% of eligible voters don't take part.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 9 — Funnel graphic: "Eligible voters (100%)" → "Vote in midterm primaries (~20%)" — Source: Bipartisan Policy Center]

A reform group called Unite America estimated that in 2024, 87% of House seats were effectively decided in primaries — by about 7% of eligible voters. It's an advocacy group's estimate. But the direction is hard to argue with.

[VISUAL: Big number "87% of House seats decided in primaries by ~7% of eligible voters" — "Unite America estimate, 2024 (reform advocacy group)"]

💬 RELATABLE: Think about what that does to a member of Congress. Their biggest threat isn't the other party. It's a challenger from their own side, in a low-turnout primary. So working with the other side can be the riskiest thing they do.

Political scientists still argue about how much this drives polarization. But the incentive is easy to see.

[VISUAL: Loop diagram: "Safe seat" → "Primary is the real election" → "Few, very committed voters" → "Compromise gets punished" → "Gridlock" → back to "Safe seat"]

And if you're registered independent, you may not get a say. Ballotpedia counts 13 states with closed primaries by law — party members only — and about a dozen more where the parties decide.

[VISUAL: US map shaded by primary type (Ballotpedia, Aug. 2026): Closed 13 | Semi-closed 10 | Open 14 | Top-two/top-four 5 | Party decides 11 (includes DC)]

### What Americans Actually Say

🎣 HOOK: Here's a question: if the two parties represent everyone, why do so many Americans refuse to join either one?

In 2025, Gallup found that a record 45% of Americans call themselves independents. Only 27% call themselves Republicans, and 27% call themselves Democrats.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 10 — Gallup 2025 party ID: Independent 45% | Republican 27% | Democrat 27%. Line chart of independents: 2014 43% → 2023 43% → 2024 43% → 2025 45% (record)]

To be straight with you: most of those independents still lean toward one party. Only about 10% lean neither way. So half the country isn't in the middle. Half the country just doesn't want the label.

[VISUAL: Stacked bar, Gallup 2025: "Independents 45% = lean Dem 20% + lean Rep 15% + lean neither 10%"]

And the frustration runs across the board. In 2025, 62% told Gallup a third major party is needed. In October 2026, just 14% approved of the job Congress is doing.

[VISUAL: Two big numbers: "62% say a third major party is needed (Gallup, Sept. 2025)" | "14% approve of Congress (Gallup, Oct. 2026)"]

> For a lot of voters, it isn't love for their own side. It's fear of the other one. That's not a healthy basis for a democracy — and it's what this system rewards.

### Why the Big Challengers Failed

🎣 HOOK: You might be thinking: people have tried to break this before. What happened?

Theodore Roosevelt, 1912. A former president, running with the new Progressive Party. He got 27% and 88 electoral votes, beating the sitting Republican president, William Howard Taft. The Republican side split, and Woodrow Wilson won with under 42%. Four years later, Roosevelt turned down the Progressive nomination and endorsed the Republican. The party fell apart.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 11 — 1912 results: Wilson 41.8% (435 EV) | Roosevelt 27.4% (88 EV) | Taft 23.2% (8 EV) | Debs 6.0% (0 EV)]

Ross Perot, 1992. Almost 19% of the national vote — about 19.7 million people. The strongest third-party showing since Roosevelt. He spent around $60 million of his own money. Electoral votes: zero.

[VISUAL: Horizontal bars, third-party presidential results: TR 1912 27.4% (88 EV) | Perot 1992 18.9% (0 EV) | Perot 1996 ~8% (0 EV) | Johnson 2016 3.3% (0 EV) | Nader 2000 2.7% (0 EV)]

💬 RELATABLE: Nearly 20 million votes. A spot on the debate stage. Zero electoral votes. That's the math, in one result.

## PART 6 — THE MAP (16:00–18:00)

🎣 HOOK: Here's the mechanism most people know the name of, but don't realize how far it's gone.

The word "gerrymander" comes from 1812, when Massachusetts governor Elbridge Gerry signed a state Senate map with a district so twisted that a newspaper said it looked like a salamander. The Boston Gazette ran the cartoon, and the name stuck.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 12 — The original "Gerry-Mander" cartoon, Boston Gazette, March 26, 1812 (public domain)]

Normally, congressional maps are redrawn once a decade, after the census. In many states the legislature draws them — so whichever party controls the statehouse shapes the districts, and modern mapping software makes that remarkably precise.

And then came 2025. Starting with Texas, states began redrawing their maps in the middle of the decade. Texas drew lines expected to favor Republicans by about five seats. California voters answered with Proposition 50, aimed at about five seats for Democrats. By mid-2026, at least ten states had new congressional maps for the 2026 elections — drawn by legislatures, by courts, by a commission, and in California's case, approved by voters. Other states considered it and said no.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 13 — US map, "New congressional maps for 2026" (NCSL, July 2026): Texas, Missouri, North Carolina, Ohio, California, Utah, Florida, Tennessee, Louisiana, Alabama. Callouts: "Texas: ~+5 R (projected)" | "California: ~+5 D (projected)". Gray states labeled "rejected or blocked: Indiana, South Carolina, Virginia, Washington, Georgia"]

Each side says it's only responding to the other. Exactly the cycle Washington described — "the alternate domination of one faction over another."

The result: heading into the 2026 midterms, the Cook Political Report rated only about 43 of the 435 House seats as competitive. Less than one in ten.

[VISUAL: 435 dots; 43 highlighted as "competitive" — "Cook Political Report, Oct. 2026". Rest gray: "Rated safe, likely, or lean for one party"]

> In a healthy system, voters choose their politicians. With today's maps, politicians get a big say in choosing their voters. That's backwards — no matter which party is holding the pen.

## PART 7 — IS THERE A WAY OUT? (18:00–20:15)

🎣 HOOK: Okay. That's why the system is stuck. Now, what are people actually trying — and how hard is it?

The best-known reform is ranked-choice voting. Instead of picking one candidate, you rank them: first choice, second, third. If nobody gets a majority, the last-place candidate is eliminated, and those voters' next choices get counted. Repeat until someone has a majority.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 14 — Step diagram: Ranked ballot → Round 1 count → Last place eliminated → Votes move to next choice → Majority winner]

Why does it matter? It greatly reduces the spoiler effect. You can rank your real favorite first, and if they're eliminated, your vote moves to your next choice.

💬 RELATABLE: It's how you'd pick a restaurant with friends. "Okay, sushi's out. What's everyone's second choice?"

Maine uses it for primaries and for federal general elections, including president. Alaska adopted it in 2020. And notice — it doesn't pick a party. In Alaska's 2022 special election for the U.S. House, a Democrat, Mary Peltola, won after the last-place candidate's votes were redistributed. In 2024, under the same system, a Republican, Nick Begich, beat her.

[VISUAL: Two result cards, Alaska at-large House seat: "Aug. 2022 special — Peltola (D) 51.5% vs Palin (R) 48.5%, final round" | "Nov. 2024 — Begich (R) 51.3% vs Peltola (D) 48.7%, final round"]

But it has pushback too. In 2024, Alaskans voted on repealing it, and it survived by two-tenths of a point. It's on the ballot again in November 2026 — depending on when you're watching, that result may already be in; check the description. And nearly 20 states have banned ranked-choice voting.

[VISUAL: Two facts: "Alaska 2024 repeal: 49.9% yes / 50.1% no — narrowest result in state history" | "Alaska Ballot Measure 2 — on the Nov. 2026 ballot (result: see description)". Map: "~19 states ban RCV (Ballotpedia, 2026)"]

The other big idea is proportional representation: a party that wins 20% of the vote gets about 20% of the seats. Of the 217 countries International IDEA tracks, 82 use it for their legislature, and another 33 use a mix. Germany, the Netherlands, Sweden, Brazil.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 15 — World map + donut (International IDEA): Plurality/majority 86 | Proportional 82 | Mixed 33 | Other/NA 16]

Could the US do it? The Constitution doesn't forbid it. But a 1967 federal law requires single-member House districts. So Congress — almost entirely the two parties — would have to change it.

> That's the wall. The people with the power to change these rules are the people who won under them. Expecting them to rewrite the rules is like expecting the winning team to ask for a tougher referee.

That's why most of the action has been in states where citizens can put measures on the ballot themselves. It's slow. Sometimes voters say no. But it's one of the few levers that doesn't depend on the winners giving up their advantage.

## PART 8 — CONCLUSION (20:15–21:30)

🎣 HOOK: Let me bring it together.

The two-party system isn't in the Constitution. Washington warned against the spirit of party. It grew out of winner-take-all math, and it's been reinforced by ballot rules, money, debates, and maps — mostly written by the two parties that benefit.

The result: 45% of Americans won't claim a party label. 62% want a third major party. 14% approve of Congress. And in most districts, the election that matters is a primary most people skip.

[VISUAL: Recap card: "0 mentions in the Constitution | 172 years, same two parties | 45% independents | 62% want a third party | 14% approve of Congress"]

💬 RELATABLE: You've felt this. Voting against someone instead of for someone. The sense that whoever wins, the fight just starts over. That's not cynicism. It's a reasonable response to how the system is built.

I'm not telling you who to vote for. That's your call. What I'm saying is that the rules shape your choices before you ever see a ballot. And rules can change — if enough people know they exist.

Washington saw the cycle coming 230 years ago. Now you know how it works.
