## PART 1 — THE HOOK

[VISUAL: SLIDE 1 — capa: The Day America Emptied Death Row · photo yt-taf016-cell-block]

On November fifth, 2026, California's governor announced that he would commute every death sentence in his state.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 2 — numero: CALIFORNIA · NOVEMBER 5, 2026 / 548 / death sentences the governor announced he would commute to life without parole]

Five hundred and forty-eight of them.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 3 — citacao: The largest mass commutation in U.S. history / — Death Penalty Information Center, Nov. 5, 2026]

The Death Penalty Information Center, a nonprofit that tracks executions, called it the largest mass commutation in American history.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 4 — numero: FURMAN v. GEORGIA · JUNE 29, 1972 / 629 / people on death rows across America had their death sentences commuted]

But it is not the first time an American death row has been emptied. It happened in 1972. And back then, it happened to the entire country. In a single day, 629 people on death rows across America had their death sentences wiped away.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 5 — linha: Ended. Then back.]

Within four years, the death penalty was back.

This is the file on how that happened. The case that stopped executions, the backlash that restarted them, and what the numbers look like today. I won't tell you whether the death penalty is right or wrong. You'll get the documents and the numbers, and you can decide.

## PART 2 — THE QUIET YEARS

[VISUAL: SLIDE 6 — capitulo: The quiet years / PART 1 · photo yt-taf016-cell-block]

Our file opens on June second, 1967. That day, a man named Luis Monge was executed in Colorado's gas chamber.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 7 — numero: JUNE 2, 1967 → JANUARY 17, 1977 / ~10 YEARS / without a single execution in the United States]

Nobody knew it yet, but it would be the last execution in the United States for almost ten years.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 8 — B-roll (Wikimedia Commons): File:Alcatraz.webm — "Alcatraz, San Francisco Bay"]

No law ordered that pause. Executions stopped while lawyers, many of them from the NAACP Legal Defense Fund, challenged death sentences case after case, and the states waited to hear from the Supreme Court.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 9 — numero: CALIFORNIA DEATH ROW · 1972 / 100+ / people waiting under sentence of death · photo yt-taf016-san-quentin]

Meanwhile, death rows kept filling up. In California alone, more than a hundred people were waiting.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 10 — duplo: People v. Anderson · California Supreme Court · Feb. 1972]

Then, in February 1972, California's own Supreme Court went first. By a vote of six to one, in a case called People versus Anderson, it ruled that the death penalty violated the state constitution's ban on "cruel or unusual" punishment.

Every death sentence in the state was converted to life in prison. It was the first time California emptied its death row.

## PART 3 — JUNE 29, 1972

[VISUAL: SLIDE 11 — capitulo: June 29, 1972 / PART 2 · photo yt-taf016-supreme-court]

Four months later, the United States Supreme Court ruled. The case was Furman versus Georgia, decided on June twenty-ninth, 1972. It was really three cases bundled together: two from Georgia and one from Texas.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 12 — duplo: Furman v. Georgia · photo yt-taf016-supreme-court]

The vote was five to four. And the Court was so divided that all nine justices wrote their own opinion. Nine opinions for one case.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 13 — lista: Why the majority objected]

Only two justices, William Brennan and Thurgood Marshall, said the death penalty was unconstitutional in itself. The other three in the majority objected to how it was being used. Juries had almost no guidance, and death sentences seemed to fall on a small, almost random share of people convicted of similar crimes.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 14 — citacao: These death sentences are cruel and unusual in the same way that being struck by lightning is cruel and unusual. / — Justice Potter Stewart, Furman v. Georgia, 1972]

Justice Potter Stewart put it in a sentence that is still quoted today: "These death sentences are cruel and unusual in the same way that being struck by lightning is cruel and unusual."

[VISUAL: SLIDE 15 — B-roll (Wikimedia Commons): File:Piranesi Carceri d'Invenzione.webm — "“Imaginary Prisons,” Giovanni Battista Piranesi"]

The Constitution, he wrote, could not permit this penalty to be "so wantonly and so freakishly imposed."

[VISUAL: SLIDE 16 — duplo: What Furman did]

The decision effectively voided forty state death penalty laws. Across the country, 629 people on death row had their sentences commuted. For a moment, it looked as if America might be done with the death penalty.

## PART 4 — THE COMEBACK

[VISUAL: SLIDE 17 — capitulo: The comeback / PART 3 · photo yt-taf016-courtroom]

In California, that moment lasted about nine months.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 18 — numero: CALIFORNIA PROPOSITION 17 · NOV. 7, 1972 / 67.5% / of voters wrote the death penalty into the state constitution]

In November 1972, the state's voters answered their own Supreme Court. Proposition 17 wrote the death penalty into the California constitution. It passed with 67.5 percent of the vote.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 19 — numero: AFTER FURMAN / 35 STATES / Florida first, within five months; then 34 more wrote new death penalty laws · photo yt-taf016-fl-capitol]

Nationally, the response was just as fast. Florida rewrote its death penalty law within five months of Furman. Thirty-four other states followed, with new laws written to answer the Court's objections.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 20 — duplo: Gregg v. Georgia · photo yt-taf016-supreme-court]

The new laws came back to the Supreme Court in 1976. On July second, in Gregg versus Georgia, the Court upheld Georgia's new law by a vote of seven to two.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 21 — citacao: We now hold that the punishment of death does not invariably violate the Constitution. / — Gregg v. Georgia, plurality opinion, 1976]

The key line, from the plurality opinion: "We now hold that the punishment of death does not invariably violate the Constitution."

[VISUAL: SLIDE 22 — lista: The new rules]

So what had changed? Under the new laws, trials were split in two: one phase to decide guilt, and a separate phase to decide the sentence. Juries had to weigh aggravating and mitigating factors. And death sentences got automatic review on appeal. In a companion case, Woodson versus North Carolina, the Court struck down laws that made death mandatory for certain crimes.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 23 — linha: From Furman to lethal injection]

On January seventeenth, 1977, Gary Gilmore, who had refused efforts to appeal his sentence, was executed by firing squad in Utah. The pause that began in 1967 was over. The first person executed after fighting his case all the way through the courts was John Spenkelink, in Florida, in May 1979.

The rules kept moving. In 1977, in Coker versus Georgia, the Supreme Court barred the death penalty for rape when the victim was not killed. That same year, Oklahoma became the first state to adopt lethal injection. The first execution by lethal injection took place in Texas, in December 1982.

## PART 5 — FIFTY YEARS OF NUMBERS

[VISUAL: SLIDE 24 — capitulo: Fifty years of numbers / PART 4 · photo yt-taf016-law-library]

Here's what the numbers did next.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 25 — numero: NEW DEATH SENTENCES · PEAK YEAR / 325 / in 1986. More than 8,200 death sentences were handed down from 1972 to 2025.]

Between 1972 and 2025, American courts handed down more than 8,200 death sentences. New death sentences peaked in 1986, at 325 in a single year.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 26 — numero: EXECUTIONS · PEAK YEAR / 98 / in 1999, one-third of them in Texas. More than 1,600 since 1977. · photo yt-taf016-huntsville]

Executions peaked later. In 1999, 98 people were executed, one-third of them in Texas. That is still the highest yearly total since the death penalty returned. Since 1977, more than 1,600 people have been executed in the United States.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 27 — barras: New death sentences per year]

Then the numbers turned. In 2025, juries across the country handed down 23 new death sentences, about one-fourteenth of the 1986 peak.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 28 — duplo: Executions in 2025]

Executions moved the other way. In 2025, 47 people were executed, the most in more than fifteen years. Florida alone carried out 19 of them, 40 percent of the national total.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 29 — lista: How executions changed]

And 72 percent of all executions happened in just four states. The methods changed too. Alabama and Louisiana have used nitrogen gas. And South Carolina carried out three executions by firing squad in 2025, the first time any state had used that method since 2010.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 30 — duplo: A country pulling two ways (mid-2026)]

So the country is pulling in two directions. As of mid-2026, 27 states had the death penalty on the books and 23 did not. Four of those 27, including California, had a moratorium on executions. And 32 states had not carried out an execution in at least ten years.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 31 — numero: EXONERATED AFTER A DEATH SENTENCE · SINCE 1973 / 200+ / people]

Since 1973, more than 200 people who had been sentenced to death have later been exonerated, according to the Death Penalty Information Center.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 32 — duplo: Death penalty for murder: Gallup, 2025]

And public opinion? Gallup's 2025 poll found that 52 percent of Americans support the death penalty for murder, and 44 percent oppose it. Still a majority in favor. But the Death Penalty Information Center notes that this was the highest level of opposition in fifty years.

## PART 6 — CALIFORNIA, AGAIN

[VISUAL: SLIDE 33 — capitulo: California, again / PART 5 · photo yt-taf016-sacramento-capitol]

Which brings us back to California. After Proposition 17, California built the largest death row in the country.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 34 — duplo: California since 1978 · photo yt-taf016-san-quentin]

But since the death penalty was reinstated there in 1978, the state has carried out only 13 executions. The last one was in 2006.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 35 — linha: California's death penalty]

California voters were asked in 2012 and again in 2016 whether to keep the death penalty. Both times, they kept it. In 2019, Governor Gavin Newsom ordered a moratorium. Executions stopped, but the sentences stayed.

Then came the announcement of November fifth: all 548 death sentences to be commuted to life without parole.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 36 — numero: THE CATCH · CALIFORNIA CONSTITUTION / 382 OF 548 / cases need the state Supreme Court's approval (more than one felony conviction) · photo yt-taf016-sacramento-capitol]

But there's a catch, and it's written into the state constitution. Clemency for anyone convicted of more than one felony needs the approval of the California Supreme Court. According to the governor's office, that covers 382 of the 548 cases, about seventy percent.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 37 — lista: Reactions]

In his letter, Newsom wrote: "I firmly believe that state-sanctioned killing is incompatible with California's values." By the Death Penalty Information Center's count, it is the eighth time in the modern era that a governor has granted clemency to everyone on a state's death row. At the federal level, President Joe Biden commuted the sentences of 37 of the 40 people on federal death row in 2024.

Reactions split along familiar lines. The ACLU called the death penalty "a racist, error-prone, and inhumane punishment." The founder of the victims' group Crime Survivors called the decision "outrageous." And the president of the California District Attorneys Association had warned against erasing those judgments "with the stroke of a pen."

[VISUAL: SLIDE 38 — B-roll (Wikimedia Commons): File:Washington Monument flags 2022-03-28 18-48-12 1.webm — "National death row: down nearly 30%"]

According to the Death Penalty Information Center, the move cuts the national death row by nearly 30 percent, to the lowest level since 1984.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 39 — duplo: Two times California emptied death row]

In 1972, a court emptied California's death row, and voters brought the penalty back the same year. In 2026, a governor is emptying it, while the law itself still stands. What happens next is up to the courts, the voters, and the governors who come after.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 40 — capitulo: Ended before. Back before. / THE AMERICAN FILE · photo yt-taf016-cell-block]

What the file shows is this: in America, the death penalty has been ended before. And it has come back before.
