🎣 HOOK: At the end of our last file, I left you with two dates.

May 2003. In Baghdad, the American-led occupation authority signs two short orders. One bans senior members of Saddam Hussein's ruling party from public jobs. The other dissolves Iraq's army, its intelligence services, and its Ministry of Defense.

June 2014. A group calling itself the Islamic State announces a "caliphate" across parts of Iraq and Syria.

Eleven years apart. How did we get from one to the other?

[VISUAL: Archival-style document card: typed header "COALITION PROVISIONAL AUTHORITY — ORDER NUMBER 1 — DE-BA'ATHIFICATION OF IRAQI SOCIETY — CPA/ORD/16 May 2003/01". Paper texture, no photos.]

You've probably heard two versions of this story. Version one: America created ISIS. Version two: ISIS had nothing to do with us. It came from the region, from religion, from Syria.

💬 RELATABLE: If you've argued about this at a dinner table, you've heard both, and probably felt neither one was quite right.

You were right. The real story is a chain. Some links were forged in Washington and Baghdad. Others in Syria, in a prison camp near the Kuwaiti border, and in the office of an Iraqi prime minister.

> Two orders didn't create ISIS. But it's very hard to tell the story of ISIS without them.

Today I'll walk you through every link. Where the numbers are disputed, and several are, I'll give you the range, not the most dramatic figure.

This isn't a pro-war or an anti-war video. You'll hear the case for those orders, their critics, and the parts of the story that happened long after the Americans who wrote them had gone home.

🔁 RETENTION: Stay with me, because the man who became the leader of ISIS spent most of 2004 in an American detention camp. And even how long he was held there is still argued about.

## PART 2 — THE TWO ORDERS (1:50–4:45)

🎣 HOOK: The orders that shaped a decade fit on a few typed pages each.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 2 — Place card: simple line map of Iraq, dot on Baghdad. Typed text: "BAGHDAD · MAY 12, 2003" / "L. Paul Bremer III arrives to head the Coalition Provisional Authority"]

On May 12, 2003, a career American diplomat named L. Paul Bremer the Third arrived in Baghdad to run the Coalition Provisional Authority, the CPA, which governed Iraq until June 2004. He took over from retired Army General Jay Garner, who had led the first postwar office.

Four days later, on May 16, Bremer signed CPA Order Number 1: "De-Ba'athification of Iraqi Society."

The Baath Party had ruled Iraq since 1968. The order declared the party's top four ranks of full members, quote, "removed from their positions and banned from future employment in the public sector." It also ordered that the top three layers of management in every national ministry be screened, and full party members removed.

[VISUAL: Document card, Order 1 excerpt: "...removed from their positions and banned from future employment in the public sector." Below, the four ranks in typed text: "'Udw Qutriyya (Regional Command Member) · 'Udw Far' (Branch Member) · 'Udw Shu'bah (Section Member) · 'Udw Firqah (Group Member)"]

One week later, on May 23, came Order Number 2: "Dissolution of Entities." It dissolved Iraq's Ministry of Defense, its intelligence and security services, the regular armed forces, and the Republican Guard. Military ranks were cancelled. Conscription was suspended. Everyone was dismissed, effective back to April 16.

Most were promised a termination payment. But anyone with the rank of colonel or above was presumed to be a senior party member unless he could show otherwise, and senior party members got no payment and no pension. The same order announced a "New Iraqi Corps," to be built from scratch.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 3 — Document card, Order 2 in typed bullets: "DISSOLVED: Ministry of Defense · Intelligence & security services · Army, Air Force, Navy · Republican Guard" | "Ranks cancelled · Conscription suspended · Dismissal effective April 16, 2003" | "Colonel and above = presumed Senior Party Member: no termination payment, no pension" | Footer: "CPA/ORD/23 May 2003/02"]

### How Many People?

🎣 HOOK: So how many people did these two orders affect? You'll see this number quoted online with total confidence. It shouldn't be.

For the party purge, Bremer said in 2003 that it targeted "the top 15,000 to 30,000 members" of a party of about two million. Jay Garner later recalled the CIA's station chief in Baghdad warning that the order would drive "40,000 to 50,000 Baathists underground" by nightfall. Later estimates that count teachers, doctors and civil servants pushed out in the following years run to around 100,000.

[VISUAL: Range bar, title "De-Baathification: people affected (estimates)": "15,000–30,000 — Bremer, Aug. 2003" | "40,000–50,000 — CIA warning, as recalled by Garner" | "~100,000 incl. teachers & doctors — later estimates". Footnote: "No official final count exists"]

For the army, the range is just as wide. A Bremer memo from June 2003 counted roughly 230,000 officers and NCOs out of work. Garner put the army at 300,000 to 400,000 men. The Guardian reported about 400,000 at the time. And some counts of everyone in every dissolved entity reach half a million.

[VISUAL: Range bar, title "Order 2: people affected (estimates)": "~230,000 officers & NCOs — Bremer memo to Rumsfeld, June 2003" | "300,000–400,000 — Jay Garner" | "~400,000 — The Guardian, May 24, 2003" | "~500,000, all dissolved entities — Charles Ferguson, No End in Sight"]

💬 RELATABLE: Whatever the exact figure, picture a city's worth of trained men, many with families to feed, told in one order that the institution they served no longer existed.

> The honest version: tens of thousands lost civilian jobs, and somewhere between about 230,000 and half a million lost military ones. Anyone who gives you one precise number is guessing.

## PART 3 — THE CASE FOR THE ORDERS (4:45–7:35)

🎣 HOOK: Before we get to what went wrong, let's hear from the people who signed. Their argument wasn't crazy.

Bremer's central point about the army was that there was nothing left to disband. As he wrote in The New York Times in 2007: "By the time Baghdad fell on April 9, 2003, the Iraqi Army had simply dissolved."

[VISUAL: Quote card: "By the time Baghdad fell on April 9, 2003, the Iraqi Army had simply dissolved." — L. Paul Bremer, The New York Times, Sept. 2007]

He also argued that the rank and file wouldn't come back anyway, that "the largely Shiite draftees of the army were not going to respond to a recall plea." In August 2003, he told PBS Frontline he had made "a decision to dissolve an army which is effectively already dissolved."

Defenders also pointed to what that army had been. Pentagon policy chief Douglas Feith called it "a pretty sick organization." And the Baath Party wasn't a normal party. It was the machinery of a dictatorship. Bremer has said polls showed overwhelming Iraqi support for removing it, and that the point was to show Iraqis that Saddam's system was never coming back. The model many had in mind was postwar Germany's purge of the Nazi Party.

[VISUAL: Three-column text card, "THE CASE FOR THE ORDERS": "The army had already dissolved itself" | "Recalling it meant recalling Saddam's officer corps" | "The Baath Party was the machinery of the dictatorship". Small footer: "Postwar precedent cited: denazification, Germany, 1945–"]

[EDITORIAL — opinion] My take: it's too easy to cast these orders as pure arrogance. Imagine being a Shiite or Kurdish family in 2003, after decades under that party and that army. Bringing back the same officers could have looked like putting Saddam's machine back in charge. That fear was real. So the question isn't whether there was a case. There was. The question is whether anyone tested it before signing.

### The Warnings

🎣 HOOK: Because there were warnings. And who approved what is still disputed.

Before the war, the White House had approved a plan that relied on the Iraqi military to help secure and rebuild the country. Order 2 reversed it.

When Garner saw the party order, he later said, his reaction was: "this is too deep." And years later, President Bush told his biographer Robert Draper: "The policy was to keep the army intact; didn't happen."

[VISUAL: Quote card: "The policy was to keep the army intact; didn't happen." — President George W. Bush, to biographer Robert Draper, 2007]

Bremer pushed back. In 2007 he released letters showing he had told the President in advance that he planned to "dissolve Saddam's military and intelligence structures." Bush had written back: "Your leadership is apparent."

[VISUAL: Two document cards side by side: "BREMER TO BUSH, MAY 2003: '...dissolve Saddam's military and intelligence structures...'" | "BUSH TO BREMER: 'Your leadership is apparent.'" Footer: "Letters released Sept. 2007 (The New York Times)"]

> On paper, Bremer signed. The White House was told. The Pentagon helped draft. And the people involved still disagree about who decided.

[EDITORIAL — opinion] I'll be honest: this part bothers me more than the decision itself. An order that put hundreds of thousands of armed men out of work, and the President later says it wasn't the policy. Whatever you think of the orders, a decision that big should have one clear owner.

## PART 4 — THE INSURGENCY (7:35–10:15)

🎣 HOOK: So what happened next? Within weeks, the consequences were in the streets.

On June 18, 2003, about 2,000 former soldiers protested outside the American headquarters in Baghdad, demanding pay. The confrontation ended with two protesters shot dead by U.S. troops.

[VISUAL: Place card, text only: "BAGHDAD · JUNE 18, 2003" / "~2,000 former soldiers protest for back pay"]

Days later, the CPA reversed course on the money and announced stipends for former soldiers. The payments came. The army didn't.

Meanwhile an insurgency was growing. It had many parts: regime loyalists, Iraqi nationalists, tribal fighters, and foreign jihadists. The Army's official history of the war, published in 2019, says the security vacuum of summer 2003 "was quickly occupied by Sunni resistance organizations," and that the CPA, "with its fratricidal Orders 1 and 2, made the situation far worse."

[VISUAL: SLIDE 4 — Document card styled as a report cover: "THE U.S. ARMY IN THE IRAQ WAR · VOLUME 1 · 2019". Pull quote: "...the Coalition Provisional Authority with its fratricidal Orders 1 and 2, made the situation far worse."]

### Enter Zarqawi

🎣 HOOK: And that's when a man who wasn't Iraqi at all saw an opening.

Abu Musab al-Zarqawi was a Jordanian militant whose group had set up in northern Iraq before the invasion. In August 2003, it carried out car bombings in Baghdad and Najaf. In February 2004, U.S. officials released a letter they attributed to him, laying out a plan: attack Iraq's Shiite majority, provoke revenge, and pull Sunnis into the fight. In his words: "If we succeed in dragging them into a sectarian war, this will awaken the sleepy Sunnis."

[VISUAL: Quote card: "If we succeed in dragging them into a sectarian war, this will awaken the sleepy Sunnis." — letter attributed to Abu Musab al-Zarqawi, released by the CPA, Feb. 2004. Text only, no photo.]

In 2004 he pledged allegiance to Osama bin Laden, and his group became al-Qaeda in Iraq. Even al-Qaeda's own number two, Ayman al-Zawahiri, wrote to him in 2005 warning that his attacks on ordinary Shiites "won't be acceptable to the Muslim populace."

On February 22, 2006, a bombing destroyed the golden dome of the al-Askari shrine in Samarra, one of the holiest sites in Shiite Islam. What followed was the worst sectarian bloodshed of the war. In June 2006, Zarqawi was killed in an American airstrike. That October, his successors announced a new name: the "Islamic State of Iraq."

[VISUAL: Timeline card: "Aug 2003 — car bombings, Baghdad & Najaf" → "Feb 2004 — 'sectarian war' letter" → "2004 — allegiance to bin Laden: 'al-Qaeda in Iraq'" → "Feb 22, 2006 — Samarra shrine bombing" → "June 7, 2006 — Zarqawi killed" → "Oct 15, 2006 — 'Islamic State of Iraq' announced"]

💬 RELATABLE: Notice that name. Eight years before the caliphate, the group was already calling itself a state.

> The orders didn't create Zarqawi. He was already in Iraq. What the summer of 2003 gave him was a country full of angry, unemployed, trained men, and a vacuum where a state used to be.

[EDITORIAL — opinion] My take: this is the link people skip when they say "America created ISIS." The ideology came from Zarqawi, not from Washington. But ideology alone doesn't build an army. In my opinion, the orders didn't hand him a cause. They handed him a recruiting pool.

## PART 5 — CAMP BUCCA (10:15–12:25)

🎣 HOOK: Now the prison. This is where the story gets strange.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 5 — Place card: line map of southern Iraq, dot near the Kuwaiti border. Typed text: "CAMP BUCCA · U.S. DETENTION FACILITY · CLOSED SEPT. 17, 2009"]

As the insurgency grew, American forces detained tens of thousands of Iraqis. The biggest facility was Camp Bucca, in the desert near the Kuwaiti border. At its peak, in 2007, it held about 24,000 detainees.

In February 2004, U.S. forces picked up a man named Ibrahim Awad Ibrahim al-Badry. According to U.S. Army detention records, he was held at Camp Bucca from February 4 to December 8, 2004, about ten months, and then released. The world would later know him as Abu Bakr al-Baghdadi.

[VISUAL: Document card styled as a typed detention record, text only: "DETAINEE: IBRAHIM AWAD IBRAHIM AL-BADRY · HELD: FEB. 4, 2004 – DEC. 8, 2004 · LOCATION: CAMP BUCCA · STATUS: RELEASED". Footer: "U.S. Army detention records, as reported by FactCheck.org"]

How long he was held is still contested. Some widely read accounts from 2014 said he spent years there. And the viral claim that President Obama released him in 2009 is false. The Army's records say he walked out in December 2004, under President Bush.

[VISUAL: Small fact-check card: "'Released by Obama in 2009' — FALSE. Army records: released Dec. 2004"]

He wasn't the only one. In 2014, the Soufan Group, a security consulting firm, counted nine members of the ISIS top leadership who had done time at Bucca. Writing in The New York Times that year, Andrew Thompson and Jeremi Suri called American wartime prisons in Iraq "virtual terrorist universities."

The problem, critics say, was the mix. Bucca held committed jihadists side by side with men who were simply loyal to the old regime. Inside the wire, they met. The jihadists had an ideology. The former officers had military and organizational skills. Soufan called what came out of it "more than a marriage of convenience."

[VISUAL: Two-column text card: "JIHADISTS brought: ideology, purpose" | "EX-OFFICERS brought: military & organizational skills". Center: "'more than a marriage of convenience' — The Soufan Group"]

> The camp closed on September 17, 2009. By then, a network that didn't exist in 2003 had had years to form behind American wire.

[EDITORIAL — opinion] I think Bucca is the most uncomfortable part of this file, because nobody chose it as a policy. It was a logistics problem: thousands of detainees, and too few good ways to sort them. But that's exactly why it matters. Some of the costliest decisions in a war are the ones nobody remembers making.

## PART 6 — THE SURGE, THE AWAKENING, AND THE EXIT (12:25–15:10)

🎣 HOOK: Here's the part of the story that surprises people. By 2010, the Islamic State of Iraq was nearly beaten.

In January 2007, President Bush announced a surge of more than 20,000 additional American troops. Around the same time, Sunni tribes in Anbar province, fed up with al-Qaeda in Iraq, turned on it. The U.S. began paying those fighters, who became known as the Awakening, or the Sons of Iraq. By April 2008 there were more than 95,000 of them, about 80% Sunni, earning roughly $300 a month.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 6 — Stat card: "SONS OF IRAQ, APRIL 2008: 95,000+ members · ~80% Sunni · ~$300/month, paid by the U.S." — Source: Council on Foreign Relations]

Violence fell sharply. In April 2010, a joint U.S.-Iraqi operation killed the group's two top leaders. Their replacement was a former Bucca detainee: Abu Bakr al-Baghdadi.

💬 RELATABLE: Think of a fire burned down to embers. It's not out. It depends on who's watching it next.

### The Exit

In November 2008, the Bush administration signed a Status of Forces Agreement with Baghdad requiring all U.S. troops to leave by December 31, 2011.

[VISUAL: Document card: "U.S.–IRAQ STATUS OF FORCES AGREEMENT · Signed Nov. 17, 2008 · Approved by Iraq's parliament Nov. 27, 2008 · All U.S. forces out by Dec. 31, 2011"]

Baghdad also took over the Sons of Iraq, with a plan to bring 20 to 30 percent of them into the army and police. Many Awakening fighters later said they felt abandoned.

In 2011, the Obama administration negotiated to keep a smaller force, at one point up to 10,000 troops, later about 5,000. The sticking point was legal immunity for those troops, which the U.S. wanted approved by Iraq's parliament. Iraqi leaders wouldn't commit. On October 21, 2011, President Obama announced: "the rest of our troops in Iraq will come home by the end of the year." The last convoy crossed into Kuwait on December 18. Nearly 4,500 Americans had died in the war.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 7 — Two-column card, "THE 2011 WITHDRAWAL DEBATE": LEFT "Critics: a residual force would have kept training Iraqi troops, watching the Islamic State of Iraq, and keeping leverage over Baghdad" | RIGHT "Defenders: the deadline was set in 2008; Iraq's parliament wouldn't grant immunity; under 20% of Iraqis wanted U.S. troops to stay". Footer: "~4,500 U.S. deaths, 2003–2011"]

Was leaving a mistake? That argument still splits roughly along party lines. Critics say a few thousand troops would have kept training Iraqi forces, kept watch on the Islamic State of Iraq, and kept leverage over Baghdad. Defenders say the deadline was set in 2008, Iraq's parliament wouldn't grant immunity, and fewer than one in five Iraqis wanted U.S. troops to stay. James Jeffrey, the U.S. ambassador at the time, wanted troops to remain. But he later wrote that Iraq's sectarian divisions weren't something U.S. troops could permanently fix.

[EDITORIAL — opinion] Here's where I land, and you're free to disagree. I don't think 5,000 troops would have stopped what came next on their own. But I do think the exit removed the one outside referee every Iraqi faction was watching. And both parties own a piece of it. One administration wrote the deadline. The other kept it.

## PART 7 — MALIKI, SYRIA, AND MOSUL (15:10–18:20)

🎣 HOOK: Now watch the dates. The next chapter starts the day after the Americans leave.

On December 19, 2011, one day after the last convoy, Iraq's government issued an arrest warrant for Vice President Tariq al-Hashimi, one of the country's most senior Sunni politicians, on terrorism charges. He denied them from the Kurdish north, where he'd gone the day before. A former State Department spokesman, P.J. Crowley, said: "The optics and timing... are highly suspect."

Prime Minister Nouri al-Maliki, a Shiite, had already been running the defense and interior ministries himself for more than a year. In December 2012, security forces raided the home of the Sunni finance minister, Rafi al-Issawi, and arrested some of his bodyguards. Protests spread across Sunni Iraq, against terrorism laws used to arrest Sunnis, detentions without charge, and continued de-Baathification.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 8 — Line map of Iraq with text-only dots: Fallujah · Ramadi · Samarra · Tikrit · Mosul · Kirkuk · Hawija. Title: "SUNNI PROTEST SITES, DEC. 2012 – DEC. 2013"]

Yes, de-Baathification was still going, now as a political weapon in Iraqi hands. Before the 2010 election, an Iraqi commission disqualified about 500 candidates, falling hardest on secular and Sunni lists. And after 2011, Maliki's government pushed out officers the Americans had brought back.

On April 23, 2013, Iraqi forces raided a protest camp in Hawija, near Kirkuk. The government put the dead at 27, including three soldiers. Protest organizers said about 50. Human Rights Watch said the government's repeated failure to hold anyone accountable had "fueled the violence."

[VISUAL: Text card: "HAWIJA · APRIL 23, 2013" | "Deaths: 27 (government) to ~50 (protest organizers)" | Source: Human Rights Watch]

> Ten years after Order 1, many Sunni Iraqis felt shut out twice: first as Baathists, then as suspected terrorists. That was the opening the Islamic State of Iraq had been waiting for.

### Next Door: Syria

🎣 HOOK: And then the border opened up.

In 2011, Syria's uprising became a civil war. That August, the Islamic State of Iraq sent one of its men, Abu Mohammed al-Jolani, into Syria to build a front group, the Nusra Front. In April 2013, Baghdadi announced the two were now one: the Islamic State of Iraq and al-Sham. ISIS. Jolani refused, and al-Qaeda's leadership later cut ties with ISIS.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 9 — Timeline card: "Aug 2011 — ISI sends Jolani to Syria" → "Apr 9, 2013 — Baghdadi announces 'ISIS'" → "Jul 21, 2013 — Abu Ghraib prison break: 500+ escape" → "Dec 30, 2013 — Ramadi protest camp cleared" → "Jan 2014 — ISIS in Fallujah and Raqqa" → "Feb 3, 2014 — al-Qaeda cuts ties" → "Jun 10, 2014 — Mosul" → "Jun 29, 2014 — 'caliphate'"]

Syria gave the group money, weapons, territory, and a safe haven. Back in Iraq, a year-long campaign it called "Breaking the Walls" targeted prisons. In July 2013, more than 500 prisoners escaped from Abu Ghraib alone. On December 30, 2013, Iraqi forces broke up the protest camp in Ramadi. Within days, ISIS fighters moved into Fallujah.

On June 10, 2014, Mosul, Iraq's second-largest city, fell. On paper, it was defended by about 60,000 soldiers and federal police. In reality, many were "ghost soldiers," names on the payroll who never showed up. Estimates of the attacking force run from about 1,500 to a few thousand. An Iraqi parliamentary inquiry later called for Maliki and dozens of officials to face the courts.

[VISUAL: Two big numbers: "ON PAPER: ~60,000 soldiers & federal police" | "ATTACKERS: ~1,500 to a few thousand (estimates vary)". Footnote: "Actual Iraqi forces present: far fewer ('ghost soldiers')"]

On June 29, 2014, the group's spokesman released an audio statement declaring a caliphate, naming Baghdadi as caliph, and renaming the group simply "the Islamic State."

[VISUAL: Text-only card on black background: "JUNE 29, 2014" / "The group renames itself 'Islamic State' and declares a 'caliphate'". No flags, no logos, no propaganda imagery.]

💬 RELATABLE: That's eleven years and five weeks after Order 2 was signed.

## PART 8 — THE VERDICT (18:20–21:15)

🎣 HOOK: So how much of ISIS really goes back to those two orders?

In 2015, The Washington Post reported that, according to Iraqis, Syrians and analysts, "almost all of the leaders of the Islamic State are former Iraqi officers." The Associated Press put the number of Saddam-era veterans in its ranks at 100 to 160, mostly in mid- and senior-level jobs. And Der Spiegel published captured papers tied to a former Iraqi colonel called Haji Bakr, describing him as an architect of the group's structure.

But other researchers push back. Craig Whiteside, of the U.S. Naval War College, studied the group's own documents back to 2003 and concluded it was "overwhelmingly influenced and shaped by veterans from the greater Salafi-jihadi movement." In his reading, the former officers were highly visible, but the jihadists made the key decisions.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 10 — Balance-scale graphic, text only. LEFT "EX-OFFICERS RAN IT": "'almost all' leaders = former officers (Washington Post, 2015)" · "100–160 Saddam-era veterans (AP, 2015)" | RIGHT "JIHADISTS RAN IT": "'overwhelmingly influenced and shaped by' Salafi-jihadi veterans (Whiteside, 2017)". Center caption: "Contested — no complete roster exists"]

> The most defensible reading: former officers gave ISIS much of its military skill. The jihadists gave it its purpose, and kept control of it.

Now line up the causes historians point to. The 2003 orders created a pool of skilled, angry men. Zarqawi brought the ideology. Bucca connected people who might never have met. The Awakening's gains rested on promises to Sunnis that were only partly kept. The 2011 exit removed American leverage. Maliki treated Sunni protest as a security threat. And Syria gave the group a second country.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 11 — Chain graphic, seven links, text only: "2003: Orders 1 & 2" — "Zarqawi's sectarian strategy" — "Camp Bucca" — "Awakening promises, half kept" — "2011 exit" — "Maliki's crackdown" — "Syria's war" → "JUNE 2014"]

The Army's official history reached one more verdict, about the war as a whole: "an emboldened and expansionist Iran appears to be the only victor."

[VISUAL: Quote card: "...an emboldened and expansionist Iran appears to be the only victor." — The U.S. Army in the Iraq War, 2019]

[EDITORIAL — opinion] So, did two orders create ISIS? My honest answer: no. But they mattered more than their defenders admit. Take away Order 2, and maybe ISIS has fewer competent commanders. Take away Syria's war or Maliki's crackdown, and maybe there's no caliphate at all. What I keep coming back to is this: the people who signed those orders believed they were closing a chapter. Instead they opened one, and other people kept writing it for eleven years.

[VISUAL: Recap card: "May 16 & 23, 2003 — Orders 1 & 2 | ~230,000–500,000 lost military jobs | 10 months — Baghdadi at Bucca (Army records) | Dec. 18, 2011 — last U.S. convoy | June 29, 2014 — 'caliphate'"]

It took Washington nearly half a century to publicly acknowledge its role. And in 2013, a declassified internal CIA history put it in writing: the coup was carried out "under CIA direction as an act of U.S. foreign policy." Why did Washington do it, and what did it set in motion?
