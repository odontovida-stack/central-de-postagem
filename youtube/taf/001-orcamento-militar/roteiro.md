## PART 2 — HOW WE GOT HERE (1:45–4:45)

🎣 HOOK: Most people think the US has always been a military superpower. It hasn't. And the turn happened in a very specific moment.

For most of American history, we had a tiny military. The Founding Fathers were deeply suspicious of standing armies. James Madison — the man they call the Father of the Constitution — said it flat out at the Constitutional Convention: "armies in time of peace are allowed on all hands to be an evil."

[VISUAL: Quote card, parchment texture: "As armies in time of peace are allowed on all hands to be an evil, it is well to discountenance them by the Constitution." — James Madison, Sept. 14, 1787]

After World War One, Congress slashed the Army from millions of soldiers down to about 137,000. We sent everyone home. That was the normal pattern: build up for war, tear it back down after.

[VISUAL: Two figures side by side: "1918: ~3.25 million discharged within 9 months" → "1922: Army capped at ~137,000"]

Then came World War Two. And in four years, the US built the largest military machine in human history essentially from scratch. 16 million Americans served. Factories that made cars were building tanks within months.

[VISUAL: Big number "16,000,000 Americans served in WWII" over archival factory footage]

When the war ended in 1945, something different happened. We didn't fully come home.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 3 — Timeline: 1945 end of WWII → 1947 Cold War begins → 1961 Eisenhower farewell → 1991 USSR collapses → 2001 9/11 → 2026: ~$1 trillion]

The Cold War started. The Soviet Union wasn't going anywhere. And Washington made a choice that shaped everything that followed: we were going to stay engaged with the world permanently. We were going to maintain a global military presence indefinitely.

That choice, made after 1945, is why we're at a trillion dollars today.

### The Warning Nobody Listened To

🎣 HOOK: Here's the part of history class you definitely didn't get.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 4 — Black and white photo of Eisenhower at the podium, January 17, 1961]

January 17, 1961. President Dwight Eisenhower gives his farewell address. Now — Eisenhower wasn't some peace activist. He was a five-star general. He was the Supreme Allied Commander who led D-Day. He knew more about war and military power than almost anyone alive.

And in his final speech as president, here's what he said:

[VISUAL: Quote card over dark overlay, phrase "military-industrial complex" highlighted in red]

> 'In the councils of government, we must guard against the acquisition of unwarranted influence, whether sought or unsought, by the military-industrial complex.'

The military-industrial complex. That phrase — Eisenhower's phrase, from 1961 — is what we're going to be talking about for the rest of this video.

He was warning that America had built a permanent arms industry. Thousands of companies. Millions of jobs. Retired generals going to work for defense contractors. Politicians whose districts depended on weapons contracts. Lobbyists connecting all of them.

[VISUAL: Simple diagram, four boxes in a circle: Pentagon → Contractors → Lobbyists → Congress → back to Pentagon]

And his warning, as I read it, was simple: the interest of that machine is not the same as your interest. It needs threats. It needs budgets to keep growing. Whether or not that serves the country.

💬 RELATABLE: That was 1961. The general who led the invasion that beat Hitler was worried about this. And 65 years later, the budget is a trillion dollars.

[VISUAL: Split screen: "1961 — Eisenhower's warning" | "2026 — $1 trillion"]

## PART 3 — WHAT YOUR MONEY ACTUALLY BUYS (4:45–8:30)

🎣 HOOK: You've heard of the F-35. But do you know what it actually costs? The answer is going to change how you think about government spending.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 5 — Donut chart: FY2026 Pentagon spending bill, enacted Feb. 3, 2026 = $839B. Operations & Maintenance $294B | Military Personnel $193B | Procurement $168B | Research & Development $146B | Other programs, net of cuts ~$38B. Footnote: "Plus ~$113B reconciliation money; plus military construction and DOE nuclear weapons — not shown"]

Alright, let's break it down. The regular Pentagon spending bill for 2026 is $839 billion. And it's not all guns and jets.

Military personnel — about $193 billion. Pay, housing, and retirement for roughly 1.3 million active-duty troops. That's real people, real paychecks.

[VISUAL: Big number "1.3 million active-duty troops" + smaller "~715,000 civilian employees (Jan. 2026)"]

The biggest piece is operations and maintenance — about $294 billion. That's fuel, training, military health care, the paychecks of around 715,000 civilian employees, and keeping bases running — including roughly 750 bases overseas. Yes, 750. We'll come back to that.

Then procurement — about $168 billion, plus a big share of that extra reconciliation money. This is buying new weapons. And this is where things get genuinely jaw-dropping.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 6 — Three-panel grid with public-domain photos: F-35 "$92M–$121M per plane" | Ford-class carrier "$13.3B" | B-21 "~$692M each (2022 $)"]

🎣 HOOK: I want you to hold these numbers next to your own life for a second.

The F-35 fighter jet. The most expensive weapons program in history. According to the Government Accountability Office, buying and operating it over its lifetime will cost more than $2 trillion. The newest planes cost between $92 million and $121 million each, depending on the version.

[VISUAL: Big number "$2,000,000,000,000+" — "F-35 lifetime cost (GAO, 2024)". Below: bar chart of latest unit prices, Lots 18–19: F-35A $92.0M | F-35C $110.8M | F-35B $121.4M]

The Gerald R. Ford aircraft carrier: $13.3 billion for one ship. We have 11 carriers. China, the next country down the list, has three.

[VISUAL: Icon row: 11 carrier silhouettes (USA) vs 3 (China). Small text: "Next Ford-class ships: CVN-79 $13.2B | CVN-80 $14.2B | CVN-81 $15.2B (Navy FY2026 estimates, via CRS)"]

The B-21 stealth bomber: about $692 million each, in 2022 dollars. The Air Force plans to buy at least 100.

💬 RELATABLE: Here's a way to put the F-35 in context. The median American household income is about $87,000 a year. One F-35A costs more than 1,000 years of that household's total earnings. One plane. The Pentagon already has more than 600 of them — and plans to buy about 1,800 more.

[VISUAL: Animation: one household icon labeled "$87,460/yr (Census, 2025)" multiplying into a grid until it reaches "1,052 years = one F-35A ($92M)"]

Now — are these weapons necessary? Maybe. That's a real debate among serious people. But what's not debatable is that they've almost all cost way more than originally promised.

### The Nuclear Bill Nobody Talks About

🎣 HOOK: There's a weapons program that barely gets mentioned in budget debates. And it costs nearly a trillion dollars over the next decade.

The US has about 5,000 nuclear warheads. Around 3,700 are in the active stockpile, and roughly 1,670 are deployed and ready to launch right now.

[VISUAL: Stacked bar, US warheads (FAS, early 2026): Deployed 1,670 | Rest of active stockpile ~2,030 | Retired, awaiting dismantlement 1,342 | Total 5,042]

The Congressional Budget Office says operating and modernizing this arsenal from 2025 to 2034 will cost $946 billion — about $95 billion a year, split between the Pentagon and the Energy Department. Mostly because we're rebuilding literally every part of our nuclear forces — the land-based missiles, the submarine missiles, and the bombers — all at the same time.

[VISUAL: Two bars: CBO 2023 estimate (2023–2032) $756B → CBO 2025 estimate (2025–2034) $946B, arrow "+25%". Icons: ICBM silo, submarine, bomber]

> Here's the thing about nuclear weapons: a 2022 study estimated that even a 'small' nuclear war — 100 Hiroshima-size bombs — could cause around 260 million famine deaths. Critics argue that having 5,000 warheads versus 500 doesn't actually make us more secure. I find that hard to argue with. But we keep paying for them anyway.

[VISUAL: Quote card: "100 small nuclear weapons → ~260 million famine deaths (Xia et al., Nature Food, 2022)"]

## PART 4 — WHO PROFITS (8:30–12:00)

🎣 HOOK: Five companies pull in roughly $150 billion of your tax money every year. Let's talk about who they are and why they keep getting it.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 7 — Five contractor logos on white. Below each, Pentagon contracts 2020–2024: Lockheed Martin $313B | RTX $145B | General Dynamics $116B | Boeing $115B | Northrop Grumman $81B]

Five companies dominate Pentagon contracting: Lockheed Martin, Boeing, RTX — that's the new name for Raytheon — General Dynamics, and Northrop Grumman.

From 2020 to 2024, those five got $771 billion in Pentagon contracts. That's about $150 billion a year — more than Germany's entire military budget.

[VISUAL: Bar comparison: "Big Five, avg. per year (2020–24): ~$154B" vs "Germany's whole military, 2025: $114B"]

Lockheed Martin alone: $313 billion in Pentagon contracts over those five years. In 2025 the company booked $75 billion in sales — 72 percent of it from the US government. Their CEO took home $23.5 million. The company spent about $15 million lobbying in 2025.

[VISUAL: Lockheed Martin fact card: "$75.0B sales (2025) | 72% from US gov't | CEO pay $23.5M | Lobbying ~$15M (2025)" — Industry: "~950 lobbyists (2024)"]

### The Revolving Door

🎣 HOOK: This next part is completely legal. That might be the most unsettling thing about it.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 8 — Revolving door diagram: General / Pentagon official → retires → contractor board / lobbyist → back to influencing Pentagon]

Here's how it works. A general spends 30 years in the military. During that time, he helps decide which weapons to buy, which contractors to award, which programs to fund. Then he retires. And within months, he's working for one of those contractors.

This happens constantly. The Project on Government Oversight documented at least 380 senior Pentagon officials and military officers who went to work for defense contractors within two years of leaving government. And when the GAO checked just 14 big contractors, it found they employed more than 1,700 former senior Pentagon officials in a single year, 2019.

[VISUAL: Two big numbers: "380+ (POGO, 2018 report)" | "1,718 former senior DoD officials at 14 contractors in 2019 (GAO, 2021)"]

💬 RELATABLE: Think about it this way: imagine your city's building inspector retired, and the next month went to work for the construction company he was supposed to be overseeing. We'd call that corruption. In defense contracting, we call it a career path.

By law, they're supposed to wait a year or two before lobbying their old agencies. But they can consult, advise, and sit on boards right away. And the relationships don't disappear.

### The Jobs Argument

🔁 RETENTION: Now — I know what some of you are thinking. But what about the jobs? And that's a fair question. Let me actually answer it with data.

The aerospace and defense industry supports about 2.2 million American jobs, according to its own trade group. These are real, good-paying jobs, often in manufacturing, which has been disappearing from America for decades.

[VISUAL: Big number "2.2 million jobs supported — aerospace & defense industry (AIA, 2024 data)"]

But here's what the economists say: defense spending is one of the least efficient ways to create jobs per dollar spent.

A 2011 study from the University of Massachusetts found that $1 billion in defense spending creates about 11,200 jobs. That same billion spent on education: 26,700 jobs. Healthcare: 17,200 jobs. Clean energy: 16,800 jobs.

[VISUAL: Bar chart, jobs per $1 billion (Pollin & Garrett-Peltier, PERI/UMass, 2011): Education 26,700 | Health care 17,200 | Clean energy 16,800 | Military 11,200 — military bar in red]

> Defense creates real jobs. Just fewer than almost any other way the government could spend the same money. The reason Congress keeps choosing it anyway has more to do with politics than economics — and we'll get to that.

### $628 Billion Over Budget

🎣 HOOK: The Government Accountability Office publishes a report every year on the Pentagon's biggest weapons programs. And every year, the headline is essentially the same.

In 2020, the GAO found that the Pentagon's major weapons programs had piled up more than $628 billion in cost growth — 54 percent more than projected when they began. That's not the total cost. That's the amount over what was originally promised. And GAO's newest report, from July 2026, says the average wait for a new weapon capability is now more than 12 years.

[VISUAL: Big number "$628 billion over budget (+54%) — GAO, 2020". Then timeline card: "GAO, July 2026: 104 programs | $2.4 trillion | 12+ years to deliver"]

The F-35, again, is the poster child. When the program started in 2001, the planned price was roughly $37 to $48 million per plane, in 2002 dollars. Today, the newest jets cost $92 to $121 million. Even after inflation, that's a big jump. And it's had significant reliability problems along the way.

[VISUAL: Before/after bars: "2001 plan: ~$37M–$48M (2002 $)" vs "2026 price: $92M–$121M"]

💬 RELATABLE: If you hired a contractor to renovate your kitchen for $30,000 and the final bill was $70,000, you'd probably never hire them again. The Pentagon hires these companies for decades, regardless of overruns, because canceling a program is politically even more expensive.

## PART 5 — 750 BASES AND WHO PAYS (12:00–14:15)

🎣 HOOK: The US probably has more military bases in more countries than any empire in history. And most Americans have no idea.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 9 — World map, red dots on ~80 countries and territories hosting US bases; callouts on Japan, Germany, South Korea, Italy, UK]

According to the most recent independent count, the United States has about 750 military bases in roughly 80 countries and territories. Researcher David Vine says that's probably more than any empire in history — Rome included.

[VISUAL: Big number "~750 bases | ~80 countries & territories" — source line: Quincy Institute / David Vine, 2021. Footnote: "Pentagon stopped publishing its full list after FY2018"]

Major footprints: about 54,000 active-duty troops in Japan. Around 36,000 in Germany — a country we defeated in 1945, that we've been defending ever since. And about 23,500 in South Korea.

[VISUAL: Bar chart, active-duty troops by country (DMDC, end of 2025): Japan 54,000+ | Germany 36,000+ | South Korea 23,495 | Italy 12,662 | UK 10,156]

> In total, about 177,000 active-duty American troops are stationed outside the United States.

### Who Actually Foots the Bill

💬 RELATABLE: Here's something that directly affects your taxes, your kids' future, and the national debt you hear about on the news.

A big chunk of the defense budget is borrowed. The US has run a deficit in all but four of the past 50 years — spending more than it takes in. Defense is roughly half of all discretionary spending, meaning the part of the budget Congress actually votes on each year.

[VISUAL: Pie chart, FY2025 discretionary outlays (CBO): Defense $893B (48%) | Everything else $980B (52%)]

A paper for Brown University's Costs of War project estimated that the interest alone on money borrowed for the post-9/11 wars could top $6.5 trillion by 2050.

[VISUAL: Big number "$6.5 trillion — projected interest on post-9/11 war borrowing by 2050, even if war spending had stopped in 2019 (Peltier / Costs of War)"]

To put it plainly: some of what we spent on the Afghanistan and Iraq wars is still being paid for by your kids. And their kids.

## PART 6 — DOES IT MAKE US SAFER? (14:15–16:45)

🎣 HOOK: This is the question everyone dances around. Let's actually answer it.

The official threats are China, Russia, North Korea, Iran, and terrorist organizations. Those are real. China is building its military fast. Russia invaded Ukraine in 2022, and that war is still grinding on. North Korea has nukes and missiles. And this year, the US went to war with Iran. These aren't invented problems.

[VISUAL: World map with China, Russia, North Korea, Iran highlighted in distinct colors; small label "China's military spending has risen 31 years in a row (SIPRI)"]

But here's where the military analysts — including people inside the Pentagon — start to disagree with the budget.

The main threat we're likely to actually face is China in the Pacific. That's primarily a naval and air force competition. But critics say a lot of the US military budget is still structured around large-scale land wars — the kind we fought in Iraq and Afghanistan.

> The US has 11 aircraft carriers. China now has three — and is building missiles specifically designed to sink ours. Some analysts say our most expensive assets are our most vulnerable ones in the war we're actually preparing for.

[VISUAL: Side-by-side: US carrier photo vs. map with range rings of Chinese anti-ship missiles over the Western Pacific]

### The Outcomes We Don't Talk About

🎣 HOOK: What does a trillion dollars a year actually buy in terms of results? Let's look at the record.

Afghanistan: we went in 2001, toppled the Taliban in weeks. Twenty years and more than $2 trillion later, the Taliban were back in power. Exactly where they started.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 10 — Timeline bars: Afghanistan 2001–2021 "$2.3T, Taliban back in power" | Iraq 2003–2011 "Saddam out in 3 weeks; ISIS rises" | Libya 2011 "Gaddafi out; country split ever since"]

Iraq: we removed Saddam Hussein in three weeks. The occupation created conditions that led directly to the rise of ISIS, the deaths of hundreds of thousands of Iraqis, and a power vacuum that Iran filled.

Libya: we helped remove Gaddafi. Libya has been divided and unstable ever since, through two civil wars.

💬 RELATABLE: Most of us were in our 20s and 30s when these wars started. We watched them on the news. We knew people who served. Twenty years later, what did we get for it?

To be fair — deterrence has worked. The great powers haven't fought each other directly since 1945. No NATO country has been invaded. For decades, the sea lanes that keep prices down at the store have mostly stayed open. Those are real things.

[VISUAL: Three checkmarks list: "No great-power war since 1945" | "No NATO member invaded" | "Sea lanes mostly open"]

The question is whether all of that requires a trillion dollars a year. Or whether a still-formidable military at, say, $700 billion could do the same job.

## PART 7 — WHY CONGRESS NEVER CUTS IT (16:45–18:45)

🎣 HOOK: Here's the political mechanism that keeps this number growing no matter who's in charge.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 11 — US map with 48 states shaded + Puerto Rico; label "F-35: 1,800+ US suppliers in 48 states and Puerto Rico (Lockheed Martin)"]

Lockheed Martin says the F-35 program has more than 1,800 suppliers in 48 states and Puerto Rico. This is not an accident.

Defense contractors learned decades ago that the best protection from budget cuts is geographic spread. If the F-35 gets canceled, hundreds of members of Congress have angry constituents losing jobs. So hundreds of members have a personal political reason to keep it funded — regardless of whether the plane performs as advertised.

The National Defense Authorization Act — the annual bill that sets the Pentagon's policy and spending levels — has now passed 65 years in a row. Through Vietnam. Through Watergate. Through government shutdowns. Through every political crisis you can name. The defense bill passes.

[VISUAL: Big number "65 YEARS IN A ROW" — subtitle "FY2026 NDAA signed Dec. 18, 2025 — $901 billion authorized"]

💬 RELATABLE: Think about that. Congress can't agree on a budget, can't agree on immigration, can't agree on healthcare. But on military spending? 65 straight years. Every single time.

🎣 HOOK: And there's one more fact that should bother everyone, left, right, and center.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 12 — Pentagon aerial photo, red stamp "FAILED AUDIT ×8 (FY2018–FY2025)" — small text: "First full audit: FY2018"]

The Department of Defense has never passed a financial audit. According to the GAO, it's the only major federal agency that has never gotten a clean audit opinion.

It has now failed eight in a row. The most recent, released in December 2025, looked at about $4.6 trillion in assets — and auditors still couldn't sign off. By law, the Pentagon is supposed to pass by 2028.

[VISUAL: Timeline row of 8 red X's labeled FY2018 → FY2025; final card: "$4.6 trillion in assets | 26 material weaknesses | Legal deadline: clean audit by Dec. 31, 2028"]

> If your accountant told you they couldn't vouch for $4.6 trillion, you'd call the police. The Pentagon fails the audit, and the next year's budget goes up.

## PART 8 — WHAT THIS MEANS FOR YOU (18:45–20:30)

🎣 HOOK: Let me connect this to your actual life before we close.

A trillion dollars is nearly 13 times the entire budget of the Department of Education. Split across every household in America, it's about $7,500 each.

[VISUAL: Two comparisons: "Defense FY2026 ~$1.01T vs. Dept. of Education FY2026 $79B → ~13×" | "$1.01T ÷ 134.8M households ≈ $7,500 each"]

💬 RELATABLE: When you hear that Social Security's retirement trust fund is projected to run dry by late 2032. When you hear that America's roads face a $684 billion repair funding gap over the next ten years. When you hear that the VA can't take care of veterans fast enough. The money exists. It's a question of choices.

[VISUAL: Three headline-style cards: "Social Security retirement fund: depleted late 2032 (Trustees, 2026)" | "Roads: $684B funding gap, next 10 yrs (ASCE, 2025)" | "VA wait times"]

I want to be clear: I'm not saying we should have no military. A strong military is real and important. What I am saying is that a trillion dollars demands scrutiny. It demands accountability. It demands that the people spending it can actually account for where it went.

Eisenhower — the general who led the invasion that beat Hitler — said it like this back in 1953, and I think about this quote a lot:

[VISUAL: Quote card over B&W Eisenhower photo — "Chance for Peace" speech, April 16, 1953]

> 'Every gun that is made, every warship launched, every rocket fired signifies, in the final sense, a theft from those who hunger and are not fed, those who are cold and are not clothed.'

That's not a pacifist. That's a man who understood war better than almost anyone alive, telling us that military spending has a real cost measured in real human terms.

You paid for this. You have a right to ask whether you're getting what you paid for.
