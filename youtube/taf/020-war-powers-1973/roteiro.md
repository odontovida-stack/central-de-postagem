## PART 1 — THE HOOK

[VISUAL: SLIDE 1 — capa: Congress Hasn't Declared War Since 1942 · photo yt-taf020-capitol-dome]

Sixty days. That is how long a law from 1973 gives an American president to keep troops in combat without the approval of Congress.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 2 — numero: FEBRUARY 28, 2026 / DAY 1 / U.S. and Israeli strikes on Iran begin · photo yt-taf020-b2]

In the war with Iran, day one was February twenty-eighth, 2026, when American and Israeli forces began striking Iran.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 3 — numero: MAY 1, 2026 / DAY 60]

Day sixty was May first.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 4 — citacao: The hostilities that began on February 28, 2026, have terminated. / — Letter from the President to Congress, May 1, 2026]

That day, the White House sent Congress a letter. It said: "The hostilities that began on February 28, 2026, have terminated." There had been a ceasefire since April. Congress never voted to authorize the war, and the clock was declared over.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 5 — xs: Congress formally declared war 11 times. The last: June 1942.]

So who actually decides when America goes to war? The Constitution's answer seems simple. Congress declared war eleven times. The last time was in June 1942. Every war since then has been fought without one.

This is the file on the law Congress wrote to take that power back: what it says, the one time a president actually started its clock, and why, fifty years later, the Senate was still voting on it this November.

## PART 2 — THE RESOLUTION THAT STARTED IT

[VISUAL: SLIDE 6 — capitulo: Two clauses / PART 1 · photo yt-taf016-law-library]

Our file opens with two short phrases in the Constitution.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 7 — duplo: The Constitution splits the power]

Article One gives Congress the power "to declare war." Article Two makes the president "Commander in Chief" of the armed forces. For most of American history, those two lines lived together without much trouble. That began to change in 1950, when President Harry Truman sent American troops to fight in Korea without asking Congress for a declaration of war. Then came Vietnam.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 8 — numero: GULF OF TONKIN · AUGUST 1964 / USS MADDOX · photo yt-taf020-maddox]

In August 1964, the destroyer USS Maddox reported coming under attack by North Vietnamese boats in the Gulf of Tonkin. A second attack was reported two days later.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 9 — duplo: Gulf of Tonkin Resolution · August 7, 1964]

On August seventh, Congress passed the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution. The House vote was four hundred sixteen to zero. In the Senate, it was eighty-eight to two. It was not a declaration of war. But it let the president take "all necessary measures." Within a few years, more than half a million American troops were in Vietnam.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 10 — numero: AUGUST 4, 1964 / THE SECOND ATTACK / an NSA historian's study, released in 2005, concluded it most likely never happened]

Decades later, a study by the National Security Agency's own historian, released in 2005, concluded that the second attack most likely never happened.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 11 — linha: Congress takes the power back]

By 1971, Congress had repealed the Tonkin resolution. In the summer of 1973, it went further and cut off all money for combat in Southeast Asia after August fifteenth. And then it wrote a law meant to make sure the next war could not start the same way.

## PART 3 — WRITING THE CLOCK

[VISUAL: SLIDE 12 — capitulo: Writing the clock / PART 2 · photo yt-taf020-senate-chamber]

The law is called the War Powers Resolution. On paper, it is short. It has four moving parts.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 13 — lista: The War Powers Resolution, 1973]

One: the president must consult with Congress "in every possible instance" before sending troops into hostilities. Two: within forty-eight hours, the president must send Congress a written report. Three: the clock. Sixty days after that report is due, the troops have to come home unless Congress has declared war or authorized the operation, with up to thirty more days to withdraw safely.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 14 — numero: SECTION 5(c) / THE EMERGENCY BRAKE / Congress can order troops out by "concurrent resolution": no signature from the president needed]

And four: an emergency brake. Congress could order the troops out at any time with a concurrent resolution, a measure that never goes to the president's desk. Remember that one. It comes back at the very end of this file.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 15 — citacao: …both unconstitutional and dangerous to the best interests of our Nation. / — President Richard Nixon, veto message, October 24, 1973]

President Richard Nixon vetoed it on October twenty-fourth, 1973. The restrictions, he wrote, were "both unconstitutional and dangerous to the best interests of our Nation."

[VISUAL: SLIDE 16 — numero: OCTOBER 20, 1973 / 4 DAYS EARLIER / the "Saturday Night Massacre" of the Watergate scandal · photo yt-taf001-white-house]

But the timing was terrible for him. Four days earlier came the "Saturday Night Massacre," one of the turning points of the Watergate scandal.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 17 — duplo: The override · November 7, 1973 · photo yt-taf020-capitol-dome]

On November seventh, 1973, Congress overrode the veto. Two hundred eighty-four to one hundred thirty-five in the House. Seventy-five to eighteen in the Senate. The clock was now the law.

_[TEASER DO MEIO — 4:10: a promessa que segura a segunda metade.]_

[VISUAL: SLIDE 18 — numero: REPORTS SENT TO CONGRESS UNDER THE LAW, 1975–2017 / 168 / How many started the 60-day clock? · photo yt-taf016-law-library]

Over the next decades, presidents sent Congress one hundred sixty-eight reports under this law, according to the Congressional Research Service. Now guess how many of them actually started the sixty-day clock. The answer is coming up, and it explains almost everything that happened next.

## PART 4 — HOW THE CLOCK GETS STOPPED

[VISUAL: SLIDE 19 — capitulo: How the clock gets stopped / PART 3 · photo yt-taf001-pentagon]

Start with how presidents see the law.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 20 — citacao: …every President has taken the position that it is an unconstitutional infringement by the Congress on the President's authority as Commander in Chief. / — Congressional Research Service, 2011]

The Congressional Research Service, Congress's own research arm, put it plainly in 2011: since the law was enacted, "every President has taken the position that it is an unconstitutional infringement by the Congress on the President's authority as Commander in Chief." Democrats and Republicans alike.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 21 — duplo: The wording that matters]

So presidents found a careful way to comply. Their reports say they are sent "consistent with" the War Powers Resolution. Not "pursuant to" its section that starts the clock. One phrase, and the countdown never officially begins.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 22 — numero: THE ANSWER / 1 OF 168 / President Ford, May 1975: the Mayaguez rescue. The only report that cited the clock section. · photo yt-taf020-carrier]

Here is the answer. Of those hundred sixty-eight reports, exactly one cited the section that starts the clock. President Gerald Ford, in May 1975, after Cambodian forces seized the American cargo ship Mayaguez. The ship had been seized on May twelfth. Three days later, U.S. forces retook it, and the crew was released. No president has cited that section since.

_[SAÍDA 2 — 5:30: a promessa do meio foi paga; risco de saída. Resolve: a frase seguinte abre um caso novo (a Suprema Corte quebra o freio de emergência).]_

[VISUAL: SLIDE 23 — numero: INS v. CHADHA · JUNE 23, 1983 / THE BRAKE / A Supreme Court ruling put "concurrent resolutions" in doubt · photo yt-taf016-supreme-court]

Then the emergency brake ran into the Supreme Court. In June 1983, in a case about immigration called INS versus Chadha, the Court ruled that Congress cannot make binding law without sending it to the president. The case was not about war. But the Congressional Research Service says it left the law's concurrent-resolution brake "constitutionally suspect."

[VISUAL: SLIDE 24 — linha: The clock, tested]

The clock kept getting tested. In 1983, Congress invoked the law in Lebanon and authorized the Marines to stay for eighteen months. In 1999, the air war over Kosovo lasted about eleven weeks, past day sixty. The House voted on authorizing it. The result was a tie, two hundred thirteen to two hundred thirteen, so the authorization failed. The same day, the House voted down a declaration of war, four hundred twenty-seven to two. Members of Congress sued. The courts dismissed the case, ruling they lacked standing to sue.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 25 — citacao: …U.S. military operations are distinct from the kind of 'hostilities' contemplated by the Resolution's 60 day termination provision. / — White House report to Congress on Libya, June 15, 2011]

And in 2011, during the air campaign in Libya, the White House told Congress the clock did not apply at all, because American operations were "distinct from the kind of 'hostilities' contemplated by the Resolution's 60 day termination provision."

## PART 5 — WHEN CONGRESS DID VOTE

[VISUAL: SLIDE 27 — capitulo: When Congress did vote / PART 4 · photo yt-taf002-house-chamber]

Congress does have another option: vote yes in advance.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 28 — duplo: Authorization for Use of Military Force · September 2001]

Three days after the September eleventh attacks, Congress passed an Authorization for Use of Military Force. Ninety-eight to zero in the Senate, four hundred twenty to one in the House. Its key sentence is sixty words long. According to Brown University's Costs of War project, it has been cited to justify counterterrorism operations in twenty-two countries. It is still in force.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 29 — duplo: Congress said stop. The veto said no.]

And twice, Congress used the War Powers Resolution to say stop. In 2019, both chambers passed a resolution to end U.S. involvement in the war in Yemen: fifty-four to forty-six in the Senate, two hundred forty-seven to one hundred seventy-five in the House. In 2020, they passed one limiting military action against Iran. President Trump vetoed both. Overriding a veto takes two-thirds of both chambers, and neither override reached it. The Senate came up short, fifty-three to forty-five on Yemen and forty-nine to forty-four on Iran.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 30 — numero: DECEMBER 18, 2025 / REPEALED / the 1991 and 2002 authorizations for war in Iraq · photo yt-taf001-capitol]

Authorizations can also end. In December 2025, Congress repealed the 1991 and 2002 authorizations for the wars in Iraq, in the annual defense bill.

## PART 6 — THE 2026 FILE

[VISUAL: SLIDE 31 — capitulo: The 2026 file / PART 5 · photo yt-taf020-hormuz]

Which brings the file back to Iran.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 32 — linha: Iran and the 1973 law]

In June 2025, the United States struck three Iranian nuclear sites. The Senate rejected a war powers resolution, forty-seven to fifty-three. On February twenty-eighth, 2026, the strikes began again, on a much larger scale. Two days later, the White House sent its forty-eight-hour report, citing the president's authority under the Constitution, not a vote of Congress. The clock ran out on May first, the day the letter said the hostilities had ended.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 33 — duplo: November 19, 2026 · photo yt-taf020-senate-chamber]

Since the war began, Democrats have forced more than a dozen war powers votes, according to CBS News. The latest came on November nineteenth. Remember the emergency brake from 1973, the concurrent resolution? That is exactly what the Senate voted on: a resolution the House had already adopted, directing the removal of U.S. forces from hostilities with Iran. It failed, forty-eight to fifty-one. Three Republicans voted yes. One Democrat voted no.

[VISUAL: SLIDE 34 — citacao: This one doesn't go to the president, so it's just a statement from Congress. / — Sen. Thom Tillis, Nov. 19, 2026, via CBS News]

One Republican senator who voted no, Thom Tillis, explained it this way: "This one doesn't go to the president, so it's just a statement from Congress." When this file was put together, in late November, a ceasefire was in place, a U.S. naval blockade of Iranian ports remained, and peace talks had stalled.
