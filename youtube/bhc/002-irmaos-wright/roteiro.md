## PART 1 — THE HOOK: NINE DAYS (0:00–1:15)

[VISUAL: Black screen. Typewriter sound. White text types out: "December 8, 1903." Then: "The Potomac River, Washington, D.C."]

🎣 HOOK: Last time, I promised you nine days in December 1903. Here they are. On December 8, 1903, a flying machine built by the head of the Smithsonian Institution, one of the most respected scientists in America, and paid for with fifty thousand dollars of Army money, shot off the roof of a houseboat on the Potomac River. And it fell straight into the water.

[VISUAL: Place card: "Potomac River · Washington, D.C. · Dec. 8, 1903". Icon: houseboat. Big number: "$50,000 — U.S. Army grant"]

Nine days later, on a cold, windy beach in North Carolina, two brothers who ran a bicycle shop in Ohio flew for twelve seconds. They paid for it themselves.

[VISUAL: Split card: left "Dec. 8 — Washington — FELL" | right "Dec. 17 — Kitty Hawk, N.C. — FLEW". Counter between them: "9 days"]

But here's the part almost nobody tells you. The race ended in 1903. The fight didn't. For almost thirty years, the Smithsonian backed the claim that the losing machine was the first one capable of flight, and put that claim on a museum label. And the first airplane in history, the real one, spent twenty years in exile, in a museum in London.

[VISUAL: Three fast cards: "28 years" | "1 label" | "20 years in London"]

🔁 RETENTION: So how does the most famous invention in American history end up in a British museum? And why did it take a contract, signed after both brothers were dead, to bring it home? That's the story today. Because every story has a before.

[VISUAL: Channel title card: "BEFORE HISTORY CHANGED" — subtitle "#002 — Nine Days in December" — tagline "Every story has a before."]

## PART 2 — THE MOST FAMOUS SCIENTIST IN WASHINGTON (1:15–3:25)

🎣 HOOK: To understand why this fight lasted so long, you have to understand who lost the race. Because on paper, he should have won.

[VISUAL: Name card: "Samuel Pierpont Langley · 1834–1906 · astronomer, physicist · 3rd Secretary of the Smithsonian (from 1887)"]

His name was Samuel Pierpont Langley, an astronomer and physicist. In 1887, he became the third Secretary of the Smithsonian Institution. The top job. In his day, that made him something like the nation's unofficial chief scientist.

[VISUAL: Org-chart style card: "Smithsonian Institution → Secretary → S. P. Langley". Small caption: "'the nation's unofficial chief scientist' — Smithsonian Air and Space Museum podcast"]

And Langley had a dream. He wanted to fly. Not in a balloon. In a machine heavier than air, driven by an engine. He called his machines "aerodromes."

[VISUAL: Word card: "AERODROME" with a small note "Langley's name for his flying machines"]

He started with models. On May 6, 1896, his unpiloted, engine-driven Aerodrome Number Five was launched by catapult from a houseboat on the Potomac, near Quantico, Virginia. It flew about thirty-three hundred feet, for about a minute and a half, and landed on the water.

[VISUAL: Place card: "Potomac River near Quantico, Va. · May 6, 1896". Big number: "~3,300 ft". Icon: small unpiloted model. Caption: "No pilot. ~90 seconds."]

Watching was Alexander Graham Bell, the inventor of the telephone, who photographed it in the air. Later that year, a sister model, Number Six, flew even farther.

[VISUAL: Name card: "Alexander Graham Bell · witness & photographer, May 6, 1896". Second card: "Aerodrome No. 6 · Nov. 28, 1896 · ~4,790 ft"]

💬 RELATABLE: You know that feeling when the demo works perfectly, and everyone in the room is suddenly sure the final product is just a matter of scaling it up? Langley had that feeling. And so did the United States Army.

In April 1898, the Army's Board of Ordnance and Fortification approved a grant of fifty thousand dollars for Langley to build a full-size aerodrome, big enough to carry a man. By 1903, by one Smithsonian estimate, the whole project had cost almost seventy thousand dollars.

[VISUAL: Two big numbers: "$50,000 — Board of Ordnance and Fortification, April 1898" | "~$70,000 — total by 1903 (Smithsonian Libraries)"]

The man who would sit in it was his young assistant, an engineer named Charles Manly. Manly also finished the engine, known today as the Manly-Balzer engine.

[VISUAL: Name card: "Charles M. Manly · Langley's assistant · engineer & pilot"]

[EDITORIAL — opinion] My take: it's easy to make Langley the punchline of this story. He shouldn't be. He was a serious scientist, and his models really flew. His mistake, I think, was assuming that if a small machine flies, a big one is just the same machine, made bigger. As we'll see, that's not how the air works.

## PART 3 — A LETTER FROM DAYTON (3:25–5:30)

🎣 HOOK: And here's an irony I love. One of the people who learned about flight from the Smithsonian was a young man in Ohio who would end up beating it.

[VISUAL: Place card: "Dayton, Ohio · Wright Cycle Company". Name cards: "Wilbur Wright · 1867–1912" | "Orville Wright · 1871–1948"]

Wilbur and Orville Wright ran a bicycle business in Dayton, Ohio. No government grant. No famous institution behind them. Whatever they spent on flying, they paid for out of their own earnings.

[VISUAL: Comparison card: "Langley: $50,000 government grant" vs "Wrights: their own earnings"]

On May 30, 1899, Wilbur wrote to the Smithsonian Institution, asking for its papers on flight and offering to pay for them. And he wrote a line that, to me, sums up the whole Wright method.

[VISUAL: Quote card, typewriter font: "I am an enthusiast, but not a crank in the sense that I have some pet theories as to the proper construction of a flying machine." — Wilbur Wright to the Smithsonian, May 30, 1899]

> "I am an enthusiast, but not a crank in the sense that I have some pet theories as to the proper construction of a flying machine."

He added that he wanted to use all that was already known, "and then if possible add my mite to help on the future workers who will attain final success."

[VISUAL: Quote card: "…add my mite to help on the future workers who will attain final success." — Wilbur Wright, May 30, 1899]

On June 22, 1899, the Smithsonian's Assistant Secretary, Richard Rathbun, wrote back. He sent a reading list, and four pamphlets. One of them was written by Samuel Langley.

[VISUAL: Document card: "Smithsonian reply · June 22, 1899 · R. Rathbun". List of 4 pamphlets, with one highlighted in red: "Story of Experiments in Mechanical Flight — S. P. Langley"]

🔁 RETENTION: Remember that envelope. The institution that would later fight the Wrights for decades had mailed them some of their first homework.

Over the next four years, the brothers focused on what Langley had neglected: control, how a pilot balances and steers in moving air. From 1900 to 1902, they tested gliders on the dunes at Kitty Hawk, North Carolina. When their 1901 glider lifted far less than their calculations predicted, their shop mechanic, Charlie Taylor, built them a wind tunnel, and they gathered their own data. In 1903, in about six weeks, Taylor built them a twelve-horsepower engine.

[VISUAL: Timeline strip: "1899 — letter to the Smithsonian" → "1900–1902 — gliders at Kitty Hawk" → "1902 — 700–1,000 glides" → "1903 — 12-hp engine (Charlie Taylor, ~6 weeks)" → "Dec. 1903 — ?"]

By 1902, Langley knew about them, and wrote to them. The Wrights stayed polite, but careful. Letters from that autumn show them discouraging a visit to Kitty Hawk, and finding reasons not to visit him in Washington.

[VISUAL: Two letter icons: "Oct. 20, 1902" | "Nov. 7, 1902" — caption "Cordial. Careful. (Smithsonian Institution Archives)"]

_[NOTE] Charlie Taylor, túnel de vento e motor: fonte NPS (checagem, linhas 17–18)._

## PART 4 — A HANDFUL OF MORTAR (5:30–8:10)

🎣 HOOK: By the fall of 1903, Langley's big machine was ready. The press was watching. And the first try lasted about as long as it takes to say its name.

[VISUAL: Place card: "Widewater, Virginia · Potomac River · Oct. 7, 1903". Icon: houseboat with a catapult track on the roof]

On October 7, 1903, the Great Aerodrome sat on a catapult track on top of a houseboat, moored on the Potomac near Widewater, Virginia. Charles Manly climbed aboard. The catapult fired. And the machine went right off the end and plunged into the river.

[VISUAL: Simple animation: aircraft icon leaves the houseboat roof, arcs straight down into the water. Text: "~0 seconds in the air"]

A reporter wrote that it went into the water "like a handful of mortar."

[VISUAL: Quote card, newsprint texture: "like a handful of mortar" — newspaper reporter, Oct. 1903 (quoted by the Smithsonian National Air and Space Museum)]

Two days later, on October 9, The New York Times ran an editorial. Its title was "Flying Machines Which Do Not Fly." It called the attempt a "ridiculous fiasco." And it made a prediction.

[VISUAL: Newspaper-style headline card: "FLYING MACHINES WHICH DO NOT FLY" — The New York Times, editorial, Oct. 9, 1903]

> The Times wrote that "the flying machine which will really fly might be evolved by the combined and continuous efforts of mathematicians and mechanicians in from one million to ten million years."

[VISUAL: Big number counting up, then freezing: "1,000,000 – 10,000,000 YEARS". Small caption: "Actual answer: 69 days."]

💬 RELATABLE: We've all read a confident expert prediction that aged badly. This one may be the all-time champion. The real answer turned out to be sixty-nine days.

Langley's team repaired the machine and tried again. On December 8, 1903, with winter closing in, the houseboat sat on the Potomac in Washington itself. Manly took his seat again.

[VISUAL: Place card: "Potomac River · Washington, D.C. · Dec. 8, 1903 · 2nd attempt"]

This time, the aircraft collapsed on launch and fell into the cold river. Manly was trapped underwater for a moment, struggled free, and came out unhurt.

[VISUAL: Text card: "Aircraft collapses on launch · pilot briefly trapped underwater · survives unhurt"]

Days after the crash, the Times weighed in again, and this time it was almost gentle.

> "We hope that Professor Langley will not put his substantial greatness as a scientist in further peril."

[VISUAL: Quote card: "We hope that Professor Langley will not put his substantial greatness as a scientist in further peril…" — The New York Times, editorial, December 1903]

And then: "Life is short, and he is capable of services to humanity incomparably greater than can be expected to result from trying to fly."

[VISUAL: Quote card: "Life is short, and he is capable of services to humanity incomparably greater than can be expected to result from trying to fly." — The New York Times, December 1903]

Langley took the hint, or the humiliation. He ended his flying experiments. Smithsonian historians today point out that his design was the real problem. He hadn't accounted for how the stresses on a structure grow as you make it bigger, and his controls were not good enough.

[VISUAL: Diagram card: small model → large aircraft, with arrow "size ×" and red text "stresses grow faster". Caption: "Analysis: Peter Jakab, Smithsonian NASM"]

[EDITORIAL — opinion] I'll be honest: I feel for him here. Sixty-nine years old, the most visible scientist in the capital, failing twice in public, in front of the press. In my opinion, that's where the later feud really starts. Not in 1914. Right here, in the cold water, with a reputation going under.

## PART 5 — 10:35 A.M., DECEMBER 17 (8:10–10:45)

🎣 HOOK: Nine days later, about two hundred miles south, there were no newspaper reporters. There were five local men, a camera, and a wind of more than twenty miles an hour.

[VISUAL: Place card: "Kill Devil Hills, near Kitty Hawk, North Carolina · Dec. 17, 1903". Card: "Wind: 21+ mph (per the Wrights' telegram)"]

The Wrights' machine had no catapult and no houseboat. It rested on a sixty-foot wooden rail laid on the sand, because the sand was too soft for wheels.

[VISUAL: Diagram: a 60-ft straight line labelled "launch rail", aircraft icon at one end]

The witnesses were men from the nearby Life-Saving Station and the area: John Daniels, Adam Etheridge, Will Dough, a lumberman named W. C. Brinkley, and a teenager named Johnny Moore. Daniels took the famous photograph.

[VISUAL: Name list card: "John T. Daniels · Adam Etheridge · Willis Dough · W. C. Brinkley · Johnny Moore (16)". Note: "Daniels took the famous photo"]

At ten thirty-five in the morning, with Orville at the controls and Wilbur running alongside to steady the wing, the machine lifted off.

[VISUAL: Big time stamp: "10:35 A.M." Then two numbers appear: "12 seconds" | "120 feet"]

In the words of the Smithsonian today, it was "the first heavier-than-air, powered aircraft to make a sustained, controlled flight with a pilot aboard."

> Twelve seconds. One hundred and twenty feet.

💬 RELATABLE: Think about every "first" in your life that didn't look like much. A first paycheck. A first sale. This one was twelve seconds over sand.

They didn't stop there. They took turns. The second and third flights went a little farther. Then, on the fourth flight, Wilbur flew for fifty-nine seconds and covered eight hundred and fifty-two feet.

[VISUAL: Table card: "Flight 1 — 12 s — 120 ft" / "Flight 2 — 12 s — 175 ft" / "Flight 3 — 15 s — 200 ft" / "Flight 4 — 59 s — 852 ft" (source: National Park Service)]

And then a strong gust caught the machine on the ground and slammed it over. It was badly damaged, and that was the end of flying for 1903.

[VISUAL: Text card: "After flight 4: a gust wrecks the Flyer · end of the 1903 season"]

After lunch, the brothers walked about four miles to the weather station at Kitty Hawk and sent a telegram home to their father, Bishop Milton Wright.

[VISUAL: Telegram card, Western Union style, typed text revealed word by word]

> "Success four flights Thursday morning all against twenty-one mile wind started from level with engine power alone average speed through air thirty-one miles longest 57 seconds inform press home Christmas."

[VISUAL: Two red circles on the telegram card: "57 seconds" → note "really 59" | signature "Orevelle" → note "operator's misspelling of Orville"]

Two errors crept in along the way: the longest flight was really fifty-nine seconds, and Orville's name came out as "Orevelle."

Notice the instruction near the end: "inform press." The press mostly didn't care. One Norfolk paper ran a story stuffed with invented details, and only a handful of others bought it.

[VISUAL: Card: "Norfolk Virginian-Pilot story: many details invented · offered to 21 papers, 5 bought it (NPS)"]

🔁 RETENTION: Hold on to this machine, because the fight over it hasn't started yet. Over the next forty-five years, it would be turned down by the Smithsonian, shipped across an ocean, hidden underground during a world war, and carried home under a contract almost nobody talks about.

## PART 6 — THE MEDAL AND THE REBUILD (10:45–13:40)

🎣 HOOK: At first, the Smithsonian and the Wrights were on good terms. Then the Smithsonian made a choice that looks, in hindsight, like it was designed to start a war.

[VISUAL: Timeline: "Feb. 27, 1906 — Langley dies" → "Feb. 10, 1910 — Langley Medal to the Wrights" → "1910 — Smithsonian passes on the 1903 Flyer" → "May 30, 1912 — Wilbur dies" → "1914 — the rebuild"]

Samuel Langley died on February 27, 1906, in Aiken, South Carolina. In February 1910, the Smithsonian presented Wilbur and Orville with its very first Langley Medal, named after the man they had beaten.

[VISUAL: Medal icon (generic circle, no likeness). Text: "First Langley Medal — presented to Wilbur & Orville Wright, Feb. 10, 1910"]

That same year, the Wrights offered the Smithsonian the 1903 Flyer itself. The Secretary, Charles Walcott, wasn't interested. He asked for a more recent Wright machine instead, and he explained where he'd put it.

[VISUAL: Quote card: "The natural plan would be to install the different Wright machines along with the Langley machines." — Charles D. Walcott, Smithsonian Secretary, 1910]

Side by side with Langley. The Wrights got suspicious. Then, in May 1912, Wilbur died of typhoid fever. He was forty-five.

[VISUAL: Name card: "Wilbur Wright · April 16, 1867 – May 30, 1912 · typhoid fever, age 45"]

Meanwhile, the Wrights were deep in a patent war, above all with the aviator Glenn Curtiss. In January 1914, a federal appeals court ruled that Curtiss's company had infringed the Wrights' patent on how to control an airplane.

[VISUAL: Legal card: "Jan. 1914 · U.S. Court of Appeals (2nd Circuit) · Wright patent upheld vs. Curtiss"]

If an earlier machine could be shown to fly, that might help Curtiss. Two months later, Walcott told Alexander Graham Bell that Curtiss could reproduce Langley's aerodrome for two thousand dollars. In April 1914, the Smithsonian shipped Langley's original airframe and engine to Curtiss's company in Hammondsport, New York.

[VISUAL: Place card: "Hammondsport, N.Y. · Lake Keuka · Curtiss Aeroplane and Motor Company". Big number: "$2,000"]

💬 RELATABLE: Picture losing a big court case, and then the referee hands your opponent a key piece of evidence, and pays him to test it. That's roughly how this looked from Dayton.

Curtiss didn't just dust it off. He changed it. New wing ribs and doubled spars. A new airtight covering. Reshaped propellers. A new carburetor and ignition. His own steering system. And he put the machine on pontoons, so it took off from a lake, not a catapult.

[VISUAL: Checklist card, red checkmarks one by one: "wing ribs ✓ · spars ✓ · covering ✓ · propellers ✓ · carburetor & ignition ✓ · controls ✓ · pontoons ✓"]

On May 28, 1914, on Lake Keuka, the aerodrome lifted off the water for just over three seconds, about a hundred and fifty feet. A few days later, with reporters invited back, it made more short hops. Later, with a different engine, it went farther, in straight lines.

[VISUAL: Big numbers: "May 28, 1914 · ~3 seconds · ~150 ft". Caption: "Flown off a lake, on pontoons, after modifications"]

Scientific American declared that "a great scientist and inventor, misunderstood and persecuted in his lifetime, is vindicated."

Then the aerodrome went back to Washington, was restored to its 1903 appearance, and in 1918 it went on display. The label described it as "the first man-carrying aeroplane in the history of the world capable of sustained free flight."

[VISUAL: Museum label card, cream background, serif font: "…the first man-carrying aeroplane in the history of the world capable of sustained free flight." — Smithsonian label for the Langley Aerodrome (as quoted by Orville Wright, 1928)]

[EDITORIAL — opinion] My take: this is where the Smithsonian crosses a line. Testing an old machine is fine. Rebuilding it, flying the rebuilt version, then putting the original parts back and implying the original could have done it, that's not science. That's a museum defending its founder's honor, at the expense of the truth.

## PART 7 — EXILE IN LONDON (13:40–16:55)

🎣 HOOK: Orville Wright was not a comfortable public speaker. So he answered the Smithsonian the only way he could. With the airplane.

[VISUAL: Name card: "Orville Wright · 1871–1948". Text: "1925 — decides to send the 1903 Flyer abroad"]

In 1925, Orville announced that he would lend the 1903 Flyer to the Science Museum in London. He believed Americans would never accept the first airplane living in exile, and that the pressure would force a correction. In his own words, it was "the only way of correcting the history of the flying machine."

[VISUAL: Quote card: "the only way of correcting the history of the flying machine" — Orville Wright (quoted by the Smithsonian National Air and Space Museum)]

In 1928, the Flyer crossed the Atlantic and went on display in South Kensington, London. That March, Orville explained himself in print. He wrote that he was sending it "because of the hostile and unfair attitude shown towards us by the officials of the Smithsonian Institution."

[VISUAL: Place card: "Science Museum · South Kensington, London · 1928". Quote card: "…because of the hostile and unfair attitude shown towards us by the officials of the Smithsonian Institution." — Orville Wright, U.S. Air Services, March 1928]

And one line that I find quietly devastating: "I do so with the belief it will be impartially judged and will receive whatever credit it is entitled to."

[VISUAL: Quote card: "I do so with the belief it will be impartially judged and will receive whatever credit it is entitled to." — Orville Wright, 1928]

💬 RELATABLE: Have you ever been so fed up with your own family that you took the problem to a stranger, just to get a fair hearing? That's what this was, with America's greatest invention.

The Smithsonian's new Secretary, Charles Abbot, tried. In 1928 he published a paper expressing regret, and offered to revise the labels. At one point he offered a new label "not in confession of error, but in a gesture of good will for the honor of America."

[VISUAL: Document card: "C. G. Abbot · 'The Relations Between the Smithsonian Institution and the Wright Brothers' · Sept. 29, 1928". Quote: "not in confession of error, but in a gesture of good will…"]

Not in confession of error. For Orville, that was the whole problem.

🔁 RETENTION: And here's a name you'll recognize from our last video. Charles Lindbergh, the most famous aviator in the world, offered to help mediate. It didn't work either.

[VISUAL: Text card: "Charles Lindbergh · offered to mediate". Small callback tag: "see BHC #001"]

Then came the Second World War. With London under threat, the Flyer was stored with other British national treasures in an underground chamber, about a hundred miles from the city. The first airplane in history spent part of the war hiding underground in England.

[VISUAL: Place card: "Underground storage · ~100 miles from London · WWII". Icon: crate]

And finally, in 1942, the Smithsonian gave Orville what he had wanted for almost thirty years. Abbot published a statement, approved by Orville, listing the changes made in 1914, and admitting what they meant.

[VISUAL: Document card: "C. G. Abbot · 'The 1914 Tests of the Langley Aerodrome' · Smithsonian Annual Report for 1942". Small note: "Approved by Orville Wright, Oct. 8, 1942"]

> The 1914 flights, Abbot wrote, "having been made long after flying had become a common art," "did not warrant the statements published by the Smithsonian Institution that these tests proved that the large Langley machine of 1903 was capable of sustained flight carrying a man."

[VISUAL: Quote card, slow highlight on "did not warrant": "…did not warrant the statements published by the Smithsonian Institution that these tests proved that the large Langley machine of 1903 was capable of sustained flight carrying a man." — C. G. Abbot, Smithsonian Secretary, 1942]

And then an invitation: if this cleared the way for Orville to bring back "the Kitty Hawk machine to which all the world awards first place," it would give it "the highest place of honor, which is its due."

[VISUAL: Quote card: "…the Kitty Hawk machine to which all the world awards first place…" / "…the highest place of honor, which is its due." — C. G. Abbot, 1942]

In 1943, Orville wrote a request for the Flyer to come home, once it could be safely shipped after the war.

[EDITORIAL — opinion] I think it's worth sitting with how long that took. Twenty-eight years from the rebuild to the correction. Institutions can apologize, but they rarely do it quickly, and rarely before someone takes something away from them.

## PART 8 — THE CONTRACT, AND THE CLOSE (16:55–21:00)

🎣 HOOK: Orville never saw it come home.

[VISUAL: Name card: "Orville Wright · Aug. 19, 1871 – Jan. 30, 1948"]

After the war, he agreed to leave the Flyer in London until the Science Museum could make a copy for its own display. And on January 30, 1948, Orville Wright died of a heart attack in Dayton. He was seventy-six. It fell to the executors of his estate to bring the airplane back.

[VISUAL: Text card: "Science Museum builds a replica · original leaves London, 1948"]

Later that year, the Flyer left London. And on November 23, 1948, the executors signed an agreement handing the 1903 aircraft to the United States, through the Smithsonian. Most of it is ordinary legal language. Except for paragraph 2(d).

_[NOTE] 2 segundos de silêncio. Música cai. Ler a cláusula devagar._

[VISUAL: Contract card, typewriter on aged paper: "Agreement · Nov. 23, 1948 · Vendors: Harold S. Miller & Harold W. Steeper, executors of the estate of Orville Wright · Vendee: United States of America, by the Smithsonian Institution (A. Wetmore, Secretary)". Then zoom on "Paragraph 2(d)"]

> The Smithsonian, it says, shall not "publish or permit to be displayed a statement or label in connection with or in respect of any aircraft model or design of earlier date than the Wright Aeroplane of 1903, claiming in effect that such aircraft was capable of carrying a man under its own power in controlled flight."

[VISUAL: Full clause on screen, key words highlighted in red: "earlier date than the Wright Aeroplane of 1903" · "capable of carrying a man under its own power in controlled flight"]

And if the Smithsonian ever broke that promise and didn't fix it, the contract says, the aircraft "shall automatically revert" to the Wright estate.

[VISUAL: Quote card: "…title to and right of possession of said aeroplane shall automatically revert to the Vendors…" — Agreement of Nov. 23, 1948, paragraph 4]

So the last word in the Langley–Wright feud wasn't a speech, or a scientific paper. It was a clause. To get the first airplane, the nation's museum promised in writing never again to credit an earlier machine with controlled, powered flight carrying a man.

💬 RELATABLE: It's like settling a family fight with a prenup. Everybody's polite at the ceremony. But the paperwork remembers.

On December 17, 1948, exactly forty-five years after Kitty Hawk, the Wright Flyer was formally presented to the Smithsonian in the Arts and Industries Building in Washington. The Chief Justice of the United States, Fred Vinson, accepted it as chancellor of the Smithsonian's Board of Regents. Hanging behind it: Charles Lindbergh's Spirit of St. Louis.

[VISUAL: Split calendar: "Dec. 17, 1903 — Kitty Hawk" | "Dec. 17, 1948 — Smithsonian, Washington". Card: "Accepted by Chief Justice Fred M. Vinson · Arts and Industries Building"]

The contract was never exactly secret. It went through probate court in Ohio, and a 1978 book called History by Contract made it notorious. In 2013, when a rival claim to "first in flight" resurfaced, the Smithsonian gave a copy to reporters.

[VISUAL: Timeline: "1948 — signed" → "1978 — book 'History by Contract'" → "2013 — rival claim resurfaces; Smithsonian releases a copy to reporters; curator Tom Crouch publishes a review of the claim"]

[EDITORIAL — opinion] So who won? Here's where I land, and you're free to disagree. The Wrights were first, and the evidence for that is overwhelming. But I don't love the contract. A museum's job is to follow the evidence wherever it leads, and a clause telling it what it may never say, even one defending the truth, is an odd thing for history to rest on. Orville earned his correction. I just wish it had come from honesty, not leverage.

[VISUAL: Slow push-in on a simple line drawing of the 1903 Flyer's outline, no people]

Today, the 1903 Wright Flyer is on display at the National Air and Space Museum in Washington. And Langley's aerodrome is part of the same collection. The museum's own catalog now calls the old label misleading.

[VISUAL: Two cards: "1903 Wright Flyer — National Air and Space Museum, Washington, D.C." | "Langley Aerodrome A — Smithsonian collection"]

A model over the Potomac. A letter from a bicycle shop. A catapult, a river, and an editorial that said a million years. Twelve seconds on a beach. A rebuilt machine on a lake.
